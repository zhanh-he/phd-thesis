\chapter{Beyond Piano: Cross-Instrument MIDI Velocity Estimation via Differentiable SoundFont Proxies}
\label{ch:beyondpiano}

\section{Chapter Overview and Position in the Thesis}
\label{sec:bp:overview}

This chapter extends the thesis's velocity-recovery work beyond piano. The two earlier velocity chapters established complementary methods within the supervised piano regime: Chapter~\ref{ch:velcomp} addressed Scenario~S1 by completing missing MIDI velocities from symbolic structure alone, and Chapter~\ref{ch:scoreinf} addressed Scenario~S2 by refining audio-derived velocities using an aligned score. Both relied on the same supervisory anchor: MAESTRO~\cite{hawthorne2019maestro}, in which note-aligned velocity labels are available because the performances were captured on sensor-equipped Yamaha Disklavier instruments. Outside piano, this anchor disappears. Public datasets for guitar, violin, and other acoustic instruments \cite{tamer2023violin, riley2024gaps, riley2024high} provide audio and aligned notes but no note-level velocity labels. Supervised training and label-based domain transfer are therefore both unavailable in the target domain.

The central question of this chapter is therefore: \emph{how can note-level MIDI velocities be recovered from real performance audio for instruments where ground-truth velocity labels are not accessible?} The answer pursued here is analysis-by-synthesis: predict velocities whose rendered dynamics are consistent with the observed performance audio, and drive the prediction with gradients passed through a differentiable interface to a renderer.

This framing leads to two candidate supervision interfaces. The first is a differentiable waveform synthesiser (Diff-Synth) that renders predicted velocities into audio and matches them against the real recording in waveform or spectral space. The second is a differentiable SoundFont proxy (Diff-SFProxy) that maps predicted velocities to note-wise loudness-related acoustic parameters and matches those parameters against measurements of the real audio. Both interfaces share the same front-end estimator and the same regularisers, so the comparison isolates the effect of the supervision signal itself.

From the thesis perspective, this chapter occupies a distinct position. It keeps the note-level MIDI velocity target and the score-informed VeloEst architecture introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:scoreinf}, but it relaxes the assumption that ground-truth velocity labels are available for the target instrument. It complements Chapter~\ref{ch:audiodyn}, which addressed Scenario~S3 with a different output (beat-synchronous score dynamics), by extending the velocity-focused work to a label-scarce setting. The chapter therefore contributes to thesis Requirement~R5 by combining numerical diagnostics, perceptual loudness correlations, and renderer-robust evaluation evidence, and to Requirement~R6 by treating beyond-piano transfer as a missing-supervision problem rather than ordinary acoustic domain shift.

The chapter is delimited in two respects. First, label-scarce cross-instrument velocity estimation has few direct baselines that target the same setting. Second, renderer-based supervision introduces a renderer-conditioned interpretation of velocity that does not coincide with a single physical measurement of the performer's gesture. The chapter is therefore framed as a practical solution under explicit assumptions, rather than as a claim about universal real-performance fidelity.

\section{Task-Specific Related Work and Motivation}
\label{sec:bp:related}

MIDI velocity is the per-note control value that most symbolic music systems use to represent how strongly a note is played. In synthesisers, velocity explicitly controls behaviour such as amplitude, sample selection, and filtering. In acoustic instruments, velocity has a clear physical effect on perceptual loudness but is rarely captured directly, except on sensor-equipped pianos. Reliable velocity labels are crucial for expressive rendering~\cite{rhyu2022sketching, zhang2024dexter}, music generation~\cite{tang2025integrated, borovik2025symupe}, performance analysis~\cite{morsi2025enable_rach3}, and data curation~\cite{zhang2022atepp}.

Most publicly available MIDI files lack velocity driven by human performance. A common practice in music software is a rule-based mapping from score-level dynamic markings such as $p$ and $f$ (where $p$ stands for \emph{piano}, soft, and $f$ stands for \emph{forte}, loud) to fixed velocity values, or defaulting to $64$ if no marking is present~\cite{dan2006psy}. Such velocities carry minimal expressive information.

\paragraph{Velocity estimation has so far been almost exclusively a piano problem.}
Learning-based velocity estimation from performance audio recovers expressive labels well, but almost entirely in the piano domain~\cite{jeong2018timbre, simonetta2022acoustics, kim2024method}. The reason is data: piano datasets such as MAESTRO~\cite{hawthorne2019maestro} provide note-aligned velocity labels sourced from Yamaha Disklavier performances. For guitar, violin, and many other instruments, public datasets~\cite{tamer2023violin, riley2024gaps, riley2024high} provide audio and aligned notes but no velocity labels. This precludes both supervised training and label-based domain transfer. The asymmetry between piano and other instruments is therefore not primarily a modelling gap but a labelling gap, and any method that aims to address the gap must operate without ground-truth note-level velocity supervision in the target domain.

\paragraph{Synthetic-data transfer for transcription.}
A common response to label scarcity in transcription is to train on synthesised audio, where symbolic labels are available by construction, and then transfer the model to real performances~\cite{maman2022general, zang2024synthtab, kusaka2025learn}. Some recent works follow this route specifically to obtain synthetic velocity supervision~\cite{sato2024annotationfree, wang2026vioptt}.

This chapter takes a different and simpler route. Instead of training on synthesised audio with synthetic labels, the front-end estimator is trained directly against real performance audio. The synthesiser is involved at training time only as a differentiable interface that connects predicted velocities to a measurable property of the real recording. This avoids the synthesis-to-performance domain gap that dominates synthetic-data transfer pipelines, at the cost of needing a differentiable supervision interface that is reliable across the instruments of interest.

\paragraph{Differentiable waveform synthesisers.}
Differentiable digital signal processing provides a flexible route for embedding synthesis inside end-to-end learning~\cite{engel2020ddsp}. The same idea has been extended to instrument-specific waveform synthesisers, including DDSP-Piano~\cite{renault2023ddsp_piano} and DDSP-Guitar-Synth~\cite{jonason2024guitar}, both of which accept MIDI-oriented inputs that include per-note velocity. Such models can render predicted velocities into audio and propagate gradients from a waveform-domain loss back into a velocity estimator. The DDSP family also includes integrated systems with detailed expressive control~\cite{wu2022mididdsp}.

Two limitations of waveform-level supervision are relevant here. First, a Diff-Synth only approximates the real instrument; its waveform statistics differ from real recordings in timbre, articulation, microphone response, and room acoustics. A waveform or spectral loss therefore spends much of its gradient budget on residual mismatch rather than on velocity-dependent behaviour. Second, an instrument-specific Diff-Synth requires a dedicated design: piano-oriented synthesisers expect piano-specific controls such as pedal state, while guitar-oriented synthesisers expect string-wise trajectories. Reusing one Diff-Synth across instruments is therefore impractical.

\paragraph{Neural proxies for black-box audio systems.}
Because standard SoundFont rendering is non-differentiable, gradients cannot pass through it directly, which motivates a neural proxy. Mart\'inez Ram\'irez~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{martinez2021differentiable} demonstrated differentiable signal processing with black-box audio effects. Barkan~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{barkan2023is2} introduced InverSynth~II, which learns a differentiable synthesiser proxy for sound matching and combines it with self-supervised inference-time finetuning. Combes~\emph{et~al.}~\cite{combes2025neural} studied neural proxies that map presets to perceptually informed audio embeddings for black-box software synthesisers. These works show that surrogate networks can bridge a non-differentiable renderer and gradient-based optimisation.

The proxy used in this chapter follows the same general strategy but is adapted to the velocity-estimation task. Unlike the audio-embedding proxies of prior work, the proxy here does not aim to reproduce waveforms or perceptual embeddings of complete sounds. Instead, it predicts a small set of note-wise loudness-related acoustic parameters that are extracted by the same procedure from both proxy outputs and real recordings. The two design benefits are clear. First, many SoundFonts can plug in off the shelf, whereas Diff-Synth requires a dedicated design per instrument. Second, gradients focus on velocity-relevant intensity and attack cues rather than on waveform details that are dominated by recording and timbral mismatch.

\section{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:bp:problem}

Let the source domain be a labelled piano corpus
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{s}} = \bigl\{(a_j, E_j, V_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^{M_{\mathrm{s}}},
\label{eq:bp_source_domain}
\end{equation}
where $a_j$ is the performance audio, $E_j = \{(p_n, t_n, \ell_n)\}_{n=1}^{N_j}$ is the aligned note-event list with pitch $p_n$, onset $t_n$, and duration $\ell_n$, and $V_j = (v_{j,1}, \ldots, v_{j,N_j})$ is the corresponding ground-truth note-level MIDI velocity vector. Let the target domain be a label-scarce corpus
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{t}} = \bigl\{(a_j, E_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^{M_{\mathrm{t}}},
\label{eq:bp_target_domain}
\end{equation}
where aligned note events are available but per-note velocity labels are not accessible. The goal is to estimate a velocity vector $\hat{V}_j \in [0,1]^{N_j}$ for each target recording such that the rendered dynamics of $(E_j, \hat{V}_j)$ are consistent with the real audio $a_j$.

\paragraph{Why velocity in this setting is renderer-conditioned.}
For piano with captured MIDI, note velocity is directly observed at performance time. For many non-piano acoustic recordings, however, no canonical ground-truth MIDI velocity is attached to the performance. The reason is physical. A guitarist's pluck or a violinist's bowing involves richer continuous control than a single onset scalar can describe, including pluck force and angle, bow speed and pressure, contact point, and articulation. A one-dimensional MIDI velocity is therefore not a universal physical truth for these instruments.

This chapter adopts an operational definition that is consistent with the analysis-by-synthesis framing. For a target instrument, let $R$ denote a chosen renderer that exposes a per-note velocity control. The desired velocity vector is the one that, when rendered through $R$, best reproduces the perceptual dynamics of the original performance audio under a chosen comparison space:
\begin{equation}
V^{\star} = \arg\min_{V \in [0,1]^{N}}
\, d\!\left(\,\mathcal{F}\!\bigl(R(E, V)\bigr),\; \mathcal{F}(a)\,\right),
\label{eq:bp_latent_velocity}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F}$ is a feature-extraction operator and $d$ is a distance in that feature space. This definition is renderer-relative. It does not claim that $V^{\star}$ recovers the performer's physical gesture. It claims that $V^{\star}$ is a useful note-wise dynamics coordinate for analysis, resynthesis, and downstream symbolic processing under the chosen synthesis chain.

This view resolves a common ambiguity. The goal of this chapter is not to infer an impossible hidden sensor measurement from historical audio. The goal is to recover a compact note-wise dynamic control that is faithful to the observed performance in a task-relevant space.

\paragraph{What makes the cross-instrument transfer hard.}
Three concrete difficulties shape the design. First, the label regime changes between piano and other instruments. Source-domain supervision is strong, while target-domain supervision is weak or indirect. Second, the meaning of velocity changes across instruments: in piano, onset energy and hammer-string interaction are tightly linked to velocity, whereas in guitar pluck style, string resonance, and finger-string coupling complicate the mapping. Third, waveform mismatch is severe even when score alignment is reliable, because real recordings and synthetic renders differ in timbre, room response, microphone placement, post-processing, and noise floor. A waveform loss can be dominated by these nuisance factors instead of by velocity-relevant structure.

\section{Cross-Instrument Velocity Estimation Framework}
\label{sec:bp:framework}

Figure~\ref{fig:bp_overview} summarises the framework. VeloEst is first trained in the piano domain using ground-truth velocity labels. The piano-pretrained checkpoint is then adapted to the target instrument through one of two differentiable interfaces. The Diff-Synth interface renders predicted velocities into waveforms and matches them against the real audio with a multi-scale spectral loss. The Diff-SFProxy interface maps predicted velocities to note-wise loudness-related acoustic parameters and matches them against parameters extracted from the real audio. During adaptation, only VeloEst parameters are updated; both back-ends remain frozen.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Chapter_6/figures/p1.jpg}
\caption{Overview of the cross-instrument velocity estimation framework. VeloEst is trained with ground-truth velocity labels on piano ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{velo}}$) and then fine-tuned for target instruments through one of two adaptation strategies. Diff-Synth provides waveform-domain supervision ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{audio}}$). Diff-SFProxy provides note-wise loudness-parameter supervision ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{params}}$).}
\label{fig:bp_overview}
\end{figure}

The two interfaces share a common training objective. They differ only in the main loss term, in the back-end being frozen, and in the output space they compare against. This shared structure is deliberate. It allows the comparison between waveform-level and parameter-level supervision to be attributed to the supervision interface rather than to incidental differences in optimisation or regularisation.

\subsection{VeloEst: Shared Front-End Estimator}
\label{ssec:bp:veloest}

VeloEst acts as the shared front-end of the framework, estimating note-wise MIDI velocities $\hat{V}$ from the performance audio and aligned note events. We use the score-informed HPT model with onset and frame as score-guidance, which is identified as the best VeloEst configuration in Chapter~\ref{ch:scoreinf}~\cite{he2026score}. We keep its architecture unchanged and use the publicly available checkpoint trained on MAESTRO with ground-truth velocity supervision $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{velo}}$. This fixed estimator keeps the cross-instrument experiment focused on the supervision interface rather than on introducing a new velocity-estimation architecture.

The estimator has two stages. The acoustic module, borrowed from the High-Resolution Piano Transcription system~\cite{kong2021hpt}, takes log-Mel spectrograms computed at $100$\,fps from $22050$\,Hz mono audio (FFT size $2048$, hop $221$\,samples, Hann window) as input and predicts a coarse velocity roll $V^{(0)}$. A score-informed Transformer then refines $V^{(0)}$ into $\hat{V}$ using aligned note information. The detailed architecture and supervised training procedure are given in Chapter~\ref{ch:scoreinf}; the present chapter treats this estimator as a fixed black-box front-end whose checkpoint is then adapted.

\subsection{Adaptation via Diff-Synth}
\label{ssec:bp:adapt_diffsynth}

In the Diff-Synth path, the back-end is a frozen, instrument-specific differentiable waveform synthesiser. Given note events $E$ derived from the aligned score and the predicted velocities $\hat{V}$, Diff-Synth renders audio $\tilde{a}$. We adopt DDSP-Piano~\cite{renault2023ddsp_piano} for piano and DDSP-Guitar-Synth~\cite{jonason2024guitar} for guitar; both are MIDI-oriented and accept per-note velocity as a native control.

The adaptation loss is the multi-scale spectral loss of~\cite{engel2020ddsp}, which is widely adopted in the differentiable synthesiser family~\cite{renault2023ddsp_piano, jonason2024guitar, wu2022mididdsp}. It is applied between the rendered audio $\tilde{a}$ and the real performance audio $a$:
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:bp_audio_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{audio}}
= \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} \Bigl(
\bigl\lVert \lvert\mathrm{STFT}_{\omega}(\tilde{a})\rvert - \lvert\mathrm{STFT}_{\omega}(a)\rvert \bigr\rVert_1 \\
+ \bigl\lVert \log(\lvert\mathrm{STFT}_{\omega}(\tilde{a})\rvert+\epsilon) - \log(\lvert\mathrm{STFT}_{\omega}(a)\rvert+\epsilon) \bigr\rVert_1
\Bigr),
\end{multline}
where $\Omega = \{2048, 1024, 512, 256, 128, 64\}$ as in~\cite{engel2020ddsp}. For each $\omega \in \Omega$, $\mathrm{STFT}_{\omega}$ uses an $\omega$-sample Hann window with $75\%$ overlap and an $\omega$-point FFT. The logarithm is applied to $\lvert\cdot\rvert + \epsilon$ with $\epsilon = 10^{-7}$ for numerical stability.

The advantage of this interface is that it exposes the front end to rich waveform gradients. The risk, already noted in Section~\ref{sec:bp:related}, is that any timbral or recording mismatch between $\tilde{a}$ and $a$ also enters the gradient. When the synthesiser is not a faithful match for the target recordings, the gradient can become dominated by nuisance compensation rather than by velocity correction.

\subsection{Adaptation via Diff-SFProxy}
\label{ssec:bp:adapt_diffsfproxy}

In the Diff-SFProxy path, the back-end is a frozen differentiable proxy $P$ of an instrument-specific SoundFont. Let $F$ denote the note-wise loudness-related acoustic parameter extractor that reads audio conditioned on the note events $E$. Both $F$ and $P$ output the same kind of low-dimensional, note-wise dynamics descriptor; their precise construction is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:bp:diffsfproxy}. Given predicted velocities $\hat{V}$, the two feature streams are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_sfproxy_features}
Z = F(a, E), \qquad \tilde{Z} = P(E, \hat{V}).
\end{equation}
The adaptation loss is a Huber (Smooth-$L_1$) distance between the two extracted parameter groups,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_param_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{params}} = \mathrm{Huber}_\beta\!\left(\tilde{Z} - Z\right),
\end{equation}
with the default $\beta = 1.0$. Each $Z_n = (\mathrm{PHE}_n, \mathrm{OSF}_n) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ contains the two note-wise parameters defined in Section~\ref{ssec:bp:acoustic_params}, and the loss averages over both components and all valid notes, weighting PHE and OSF equally ($1{:}1$). The Huber form tolerates outliers from onset contamination such as overlapping notes, while the parameter-space objective concentrates gradients on note intensity and attack behaviour rather than on waveform details that are weakly related to velocity.

The asymmetry between Eq.~\eqref{eq:bp_audio_loss} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:bp_param_loss} is the central design point of this chapter. The Diff-Synth path optimises in waveform/spectral space, where velocity is one of many factors influencing the signal. The Diff-SFProxy path optimises in a low-dimensional note-wise dynamics space, where velocity is the dominant factor by construction. The remainder of the chapter tests whether this difference matters in practice.

\subsection{Shared Regularisers and Total Objective}
\label{ssec:bp:regularisers}

Both adaptation paths share two auxiliary regularisers on the predicted onset-velocity distribution $\hat{\mathbf{v}} \in [0,1]^{N}$, where $N$ is the number of valid onsets in a batch. The first is an anti-collapse prior,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_anti_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anti}}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}) = (\bar{v} - \mu)^2
+ \max\!\left(0,\ \sigma_{\min}^2 - \mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mathbf{v}})\right),
\end{equation}
where $\bar{v}$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\hat{\mathbf{v}})$ are the empirical mean and variance over onset positions, $\mu = 0.5$ is a desired operating point, and $\sigma_{\min}^2 = 0.01$ is a minimum-variance threshold. The mean term resists drift towards extremes. The hinge variance term penalises collapse towards a single output value.

The second is a soft saturation penalty,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_satu_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{satu}}(\hat{\mathbf{v}}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
\bigl[\max(0,\, \hat{v}_n - \tau)\bigr]^2,
\end{equation}
which quadratically penalises predictions above a near-maximum threshold $\tau = 0.95$. This discourages VeloEst from pushing mass into the saturated region, where audio-derived gradients become uninformative because further increases in velocity produce diminishing changes in the rendered output.

The total adaptation objective combines the active main loss with the two regularisers,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_total_loss}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{main}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{anti}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anti}}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{satu}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{satu}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{main}} \in \{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{audio}},\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{params}}\}$ selects the active interface and the regulariser weights are zero outside adaptation. For both adaptation branches the fixed weight ratio $1 : 0.2 : 0.4$ is used, found by grid search and held constant across all experiments.

\section{Differentiable SoundFont Proxy}
\label{sec:bp:diffsfproxy}

This section defines the loudness-related acoustic parameters that form the proxy's output space, the proxy architecture, and the proxy's offline training procedure. The proxy is trained once for each instrument-SoundFont pair and then frozen throughout the adaptation experiments in Sections~\ref{sec:bp:proxy_diagnostic} and~\ref{sec:bp:adaptation_results}.

\subsection{Loudness-Related Acoustic Parameters}
\label{ssec:bp:acoustic_params}

To characterise the velocity-dependent acoustic behaviour of a SoundFont, two note-wise parameters are extracted from rendered or real audio. The extractor $F$ computes both parameters from the magnitude STFT $X(k,t) = \lvert\mathrm{STFT}(k,t)\rvert$, using the same FFT settings as Section~\ref{ssec:bp:veloest}. Here $k$ indexes frequency bins and $t$ indexes STFT frames. For each valid note $n \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$, the extractor stacks the parameters into
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_Z_def}
Z_n = \bigl(\mathrm{PHE}_n,\ \mathrm{OSF}_n\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^2,
\end{equation}
and stacks all note-wise pairs into $Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_N) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}$. The two parameters are designed to capture the early, velocity-sensitive portion of each note while remaining robust to alignment slack and to mismatch between SoundFont and real recordings.

\paragraph{Pitch-conditioned harmonic energy (PHE).}
The first parameter draws on harmonic-structure-based note-intensity estimation~\cite{ewert2011est}. It accumulates magnitude energy around the retained harmonics of the note pitch:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_phe}
\mathrm{PHE}_n =
\log\!\left(1 + \sum_{h=1}^{H_n}
\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{B}_{n,h}\rvert\,\lvert\mathcal{T}_n\rvert}
\sum_{k \in \mathcal{B}_{n,h}} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}_n} X(k,t)^2 \right),
\end{equation}
where $h$ indexes harmonics, $\mathcal{B}_{n,h}$ is the $\pm 1$-bin frequency band around the $h$-th harmonic of note $n$, $\mathcal{T}_n$ is the set of STFT frames within $120$\,ms after the note onset, and $H_n \le 5$ is the number of retained harmonics below Nyquist. The narrow band handles STFT leakage while rejecting nearby pitches. The five-harmonic cap keeps PHE on lower partials that are less exposed to inharmonicity, tuning or rendering mismatch, and overlap.

\paragraph{Onset-window spectral flux (OSF).}
The second parameter is based on spectral-flux onset functions, which are used in onset detection by capturing positive spectral differences~\cite{dixon2006onset, boeck2013maximum}. It measures the broadband, half-wave-rectified spectral increase around the note onset:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bp_osf}
\mathrm{OSF}_n =
\log\!\left(1 + \frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{O}_n\rvert}
\sum_{t \in \mathcal{O}_n} \sum_k \bigl[ X(k,t) - X(k,t-1) \bigr]_+ \right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_n$ is the set of STFT frames from $20$\,ms before to $80$\,ms after the note onset, and $[u]_+ = \max(u, 0)$ for any scalar $u$. The short pre-onset side absorbs alignment slack and provides a local reference. The longer post-onset side covers the main struck or plucked-string transient without folding in sustained energy.

These two parameters are intentionally narrow. They do not aim to model full loudness perception. They focus on local note-wise cues that are strongly influenced by velocity in many synthesis chains, and they form the supervision signal that the proxy is required to predict.

\subsection{Proxy Architecture}
\label{ssec:bp:proxy_arch}

Figure~\ref{fig:bp_proxy_arch} shows the proxy architecture. A Transformer encoder maps a padded sequence of up to $N_{\max} = 64$ note events in a $5$\,s segment to the corresponding $(\widetilde{\mathrm{PHE}}_n, \widetilde{\mathrm{OSF}}_n)$ pairs. The choice $N_{\max} = 64$ is motivated by a data analysis on MAESTRO showing that human performances rarely exceed $64$ notes per $5$\,s.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\columnwidth]{Chapter_6/figures/p2.jpg}
\caption{An encoder-only Transformer maps padded note sequences to per-note PHE/OSF targets extracted from SoundFont-rendered audio.}
\label{fig:bp_proxy_arch}
\end{figure}

Each note token combines three components. The first is a learnable pitch embedding. The second is a shared learnable basis that maps onset, duration, and velocity (each normalised to $[0,1]$) to additive directions in token space. The third is a two-layer MLP with GELU activation on the normalised onset, acting as a learnable positional encoding. These components are summed and passed through LayerNorm to form the note token. Padded slots are then zeroed.

The encoder stacks six pre-norm Transformer layers ($d = 256$, $8$ heads, FFN width $4d = 1024$, GELU, dropout $0.1$), with a key-padding mask excluding padded slots from attention. A shared note-wise head ($d \to d \to 2$, with GELU) emits each $(\widetilde{\mathrm{PHE}}_n, \widetilde{\mathrm{OSF}}_n)$ pair. The encoder-only design fits this set-to-set regression task naturally, because the proxy has no autoregressive generation requirement: each note's targets depend on the full event context but are produced in parallel. All three segment-length variants of the proxy ($2/5/10$\,s, with $N_{\max}$ set to $32/64/128$ accordingly) share the same architecture and yield approximately $4.9$\,M parameters.

\subsection{MIDI Segment Sampling}
\label{ssec:bp:midi_sampling}

The proxy is trained entirely on procedurally generated MIDI segments. Each segment is a list of note events $\{(p_n, t_n, \ell_n, v_n)\}_{n=1}^{N}$ in which pitch, onset, duration, and velocity $v_n \in [0,1]$ are all written by the sampler rather than measured from any recording. The velocities therefore serve both as the proxy's input control variable during training and as the recoverable target in the velocity-recovery diagnostic of Section~\ref{sec:bp:proxy_diagnostic}; this is the only sense in which a per-note velocity ``ground truth'' exists in this chapter.

Note events come from a three-component mixture, weighted $0.5 / 0.3 / 0.2$.

\paragraph{Coverage component ($50\%$).}
Ordinary polyphonic textures: pitches uniform over the MIDI piano range $[21, 108]$, polyphony capped at $14$, durations uniform in $[0.08, 0.80]$\,s, and inter-onset intervals uniform in $[0.03, 0.35]$\,s. Per-note velocities are stratified between uniform, near-boundary, and extreme values.

\paragraph{Boundary component ($30\%$).}
A low-polyphony pass (cap of $2$, chord probability $0.12$) with velocities biased towards the SoundFont's velocity-layer boundaries. SoundFonts internally store a small number of discrete sample layers, one per velocity range, and a small velocity change near a layer boundary produces a \emph{sharp} acoustic transition rather than a smooth gain change. The proxy must learn these transitions, and this component concentrates training mass on them.

\paragraph{Stress component ($20\%$).}
Deliberately adversarial: polyphony up to $16$, chord size up to $8$, inter-onset intervals as short as $0.01$\,s, and velocities biased towards the extremes of $[0, 1]$.

Within any chord, in all three components, per-note velocities are sampled independently, so the proxy is trained to produce per-note rather than chord-uniform velocity responses.

\subsection{Teacher Rendering and Training Objective}
\label{ssec:bp:proxy_training}

Each sampled segment $(E, V)$ is rendered to audio with the SoundFont and then reduced to per-note PHE/OSF as the teacher target.

\paragraph{Teacher rendering.}
Audio is rendered using the chosen SoundFont (\texttt{.sfz}) via the Python package \texttt{sfizz} at $22050$\,Hz, in segments of $2$, $5$, or $10$\,s with reverb disabled and short fade-outs to suppress synthesiser state leakage. The extractor $F$ from Section~\ref{ssec:bp:acoustic_params}, conditioned on the original note events, then produces the per-note target $Z = (\mathrm{PHE}_n, \mathrm{OSF}_n)_{n=1}^{N}$. Rendered segments and their targets are cached once and reused across epochs.

\paragraph{Training objective.}
Given input $(E, V)$, the proxy predicts $\widetilde{Z} = (\widetilde{\mathrm{PHE}}_n, \widetilde{\mathrm{OSF}}_n)_{n=1}^{N}$. We train it by minimising the Huber loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{params}}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bp_param_loss}, with a strict mask excluding padded slots from the gradient computation.

\paragraph{One loss form, two stages.}
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{params}}$ appears at two distinct stages of the framework, and it is helpful to keep them apart. In \emph{proxy training} (this section), the proxy parameters are updated; the loss matches proxy prediction $P(E, V)$ against the teacher target $F(R(E, V))$ extracted from SoundFont-rendered audio. In \emph{adaptation} (Section~\ref{ssec:bp:adapt_diffsfproxy}), the proxy is frozen and VeloEst parameters are updated; the loss matches $P(E, \hat{V})$ against $F(a, E)$ extracted from \emph{real} audio. Same functional form, different operands, different parameters being updated.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:bp:experiments}

The experiments are organised around two questions. First, can the frozen Diff-SFProxy provide useful gradients for velocity recovery when the true velocity is known in synthetic data? This is a precondition for the next experiment and is reported in Section~\ref{sec:bp:proxy_diagnostic}. Second, when the front-end VeloEst model is adapted using real target-domain audio, does Diff-SFProxy provide a better supervision interface than waveform-domain Diff-Synth? This is the main empirical question of the chapter, reported in Section~\ref{sec:bp:adaptation_results}. The two experiments use different evaluation metrics: the diagnostic uses synthetic per-note velocity MAE, defined inline in Section~\ref{ssec:bp:proxy_diagnostic_protocol}, whereas the adaptation experiment uses perceptual loudness metrics, defined in Section~\ref{ssec:bp:metrics}. The present section defines only the datasets and the SoundFont assets shared by both experiments.

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{ssec:bp:datasets}

The experiments use piano as the source domain, where velocity labels are available, and guitar as the main label-scarce target domain.

For piano, training and in-domain evaluation use the MAESTRO V3 dataset~\cite{hawthorne2019maestro}, which contains $198.7$\,h of Yamaha Disklavier performances with note-aligned MIDI velocities. The default train/valid/test split is followed throughout. Cross-dataset evaluation uses the SMD dataset~\cite{smd}, which contains $4.7$\,h of additional Yamaha Disklavier performances with the same label format.

For guitar, adaptation uses the GAPS dataset~\cite{riley2024gaps}, which contains $14$\,h of nylon-string classical guitar with aligned notes but no velocity labels, again using its default train/valid/test split. Cross-dataset evaluation uses the Fran\c{c}ois Leduc (FL) dataset~\cite{riley2024high}, which contains $4.0$\,h of solo jazz guitar recordings on a mix of nylon-string, steel-string, electric, and acoustic guitars. The two guitar corpora differ substantially in playing style, recording chain, and instrument variety, which makes FL a useful test of out-of-distribution behaviour for guitar adaptation.

This dataset configuration has a deliberate asymmetry. On piano, ground-truth velocities are available on both the adaptation and the cross-dataset evaluation set, which permits direct error metrics. On guitar, no ground-truth velocities are available on either set, which forces a different evaluation protocol based on perceptual loudness comparison (Section~\ref{ssec:bp:metrics}).

\subsection{SoundFont Selection}
\label{ssec:bp:soundfonts}

The SoundFont is chosen deliberately for each target instrument because a mismatched timbre would produce a teacher signal that the target recordings cannot match, regardless of how well the proxy is trained. Candidates were auditioned from the public FreePats sound banks, and the closest match was selected for each instrument family. Salamander Grand Piano~\cite{sf_salamander} is used for piano, since it approximates the Yamaha grand piano timbre of MAESTRO and SMD. Spanish Classical Guitar~\cite{sf_spanishguitar} is used for guitar, since it is a close match for GAPS and for the nylon-string portion of FL.

For the metric diagnostic in Section~\ref{ssec:bp:metric_diagnostic}, an alternative piano SoundFont is also used: YDP Grand Piano~\cite{sf_ydpgrand}, an earlier and less realistic Yamaha grand piano sample. This second SoundFont is not used to train any proxy. It is used only to test how sensitive the chosen evaluation metric is to a change of rendering asset.

\section{Diff-SFProxy Velocity-Recovery Diagnostic}
\label{sec:bp:proxy_diagnostic}

Before deploying Diff-SFProxy in cross-instrument adaptation, we test whether its gradients can be trusted. The question is direct: \emph{do the proxy's gradients point velocity in the right direction?} The answer is obtained through a controlled inversion on synthetic audio, where the true velocities are known by construction. The test segments are drawn from the same procedural sampler as the proxy's training data (Section~\ref{ssec:bp:midi_sampling}), so the velocities are written into each segment by the sampler rather than measured from any recording. This holds for any instrument-SoundFont pair, including guitar, which is why a per-note velocity MAE is reported for both piano and guitar proxies in Table~\ref{tab:bp_proxy_recovery}. The setting also isolates the gradient pathway from any real-audio domain gap. A pass on this diagnostic is a precondition for the cross-instrument adaptation experiments in Section~\ref{sec:bp:adaptation_results}.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{ssec:bp:proxy_training_protocol}

Each proxy is trained on $20$k MIDI segments, with an additional $2$k for validation. The optimiser is AdamW with a learning rate of $10^{-4}$, weight decay $0.01$, batch size $64$, and a fixed random seed of $19$. Diff-SFProxy is trained for $200$ epochs; the validation loss typically plateaus between epochs $150$ and $200$. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is selected for each run. To accommodate the three input segment lengths $2/5/10$\,s, $N_{\max}$ is set to $32/64/128$, respectively. All three proxies use the same Transformer architecture, resulting in approximately $4.9$\,M parameters each.

\subsection{Diagnostic Protocol}
\label{ssec:bp:proxy_diagnostic_protocol}

Synthetic MIDI segments are rendered with the chosen SoundFont, and their PHE/OSF parameters are extracted as the target. All note velocities are then reset to $64$, the proxy is frozen, and gradient descent is run on the candidate velocities until the proxy's predicted PHE/OSF match the target. The optimiser is Adam with $120$ steps and a learning rate of $0.03$. The headline metric is the per-note velocity MAE, on the MIDI scale $[0, 127]$, between recovered and true velocities. A substantial reduction from the initial error indicates that the proxy's gradients yield update directions towards the true velocities, which is exactly the property needed for downstream adaptation.

Two test groups are used, each containing $2$k MIDI segments. The \textbf{in-domain test} draws from the same sampler used for training. The \textbf{stress test} uses denser polyphony, larger chords, and tighter inter-onset intervals to probe behaviour beyond the training distribution.

\subsection{Results}
\label{ssec:bp:proxy_results}

Table~\ref{tab:bp_proxy_recovery} reports the diagnostic for piano and guitar proxies at $2/5/10$\,s segment lengths. Three patterns are visible. First, in-domain recovery cuts the initial error to about a third for all six proxies, both for piano (gain around $67$--$68\%$) and for guitar (gain around $72$--$74\%$). Second, under the stress test the recovery improves from $2$\,s to $5$\,s, most clearly on piano (gain rises from $49.5\%$ to $54.0\%$), suggesting that a longer context helps the proxy disentangle dense overlapping notes. The $10$\,s setting therefore adds training cost without further benefit and is discarded in the main study. \section{Cross-Instrument VeloEst Adaptation}
\label{sec:bp:adaptation_results}

The adaptation experiment serves two distinct purposes. On guitar, where velocity labels are unavailable, it provides a label-free route to MIDI velocity estimation and allows the practical question of beyond-piano deployment to be answered directly. On piano, where ground-truth velocities are available on MAESTRO and SMD, it serves as a controlled diagnostic. We adapt on MAESTRO so that predictions can be checked against true labels, verifying whether the non-velocity supervision pushes velocity towards the right value. The piano result is therefore not a claim about improving over a supervised piano baseline; it is a sanity check that the supervision signal is informative.

The label scarcity referred to here is on real recordings. GAPS and FL datasets provide aligned note events, but the per-note velocities used by the performers were never captured by an instrument-side sensor and are therefore not accessible as supervision. This is the opposite of the synthetic guitar segments used in Section~\ref{sec:bp:proxy_diagnostic}, where the velocity of every note is written into the MIDI segment by the sampler itself before any rendering takes place, and is therefore directly available for per-note MAE comparison. Real-recording label scarcity is a property of how the dataset was captured, not of the instrument itself.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{ssec:bp:adapt_train}

Three settings are compared on the same VeloEst front-end: the piano-pretrained baseline applied without further training, Diff-Synth adaptation, and Diff-SFProxy adaptation. The Diff-Synth back-ends~\cite{renault2023ddsp_piano, jonason2024guitar} were originally implemented for $3$\,s input segments. Each is retrained at $2$\,s and $5$\,s with its original hyperparameters, then frozen, so that crop length is comparable across the two adaptation interfaces.

During adaptation, VeloEst processes $10$\,s segments and outputs a frame-wise velocity roll. The frozen back-end processes shorter crops, using $5$\,s by default and $2$\,s for ablation. Back-end losses are averaged over valid notes in each crop and back-propagated through the onset-frame velocity reads to the full velocity roll, which keeps the gradient scale comparable across crop lengths. Adaptation is run separately on the MAESTRO and GAPS training sets for $120$k iterations using Adam with learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch size $8$, and step decay $0.9$ every $10$k iterations. SMD and FL datasets are used only for evaluation. All runs use seed $19$, and the final checkpoint is selected by validation $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ on MAESTRO for piano and on GAPS for guitar.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{ssec:bp:metrics}

For piano datasets with ground-truth velocities, we follow~\cite{kim2024method} and report per-note $\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{Velo}}$ on the MIDI scale $[0, 127]$, computed against the true note velocities. For datasets without velocity labels, $\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{Velo}}$ cannot be computed. We instead re-render the aligned notes through the selected SoundFont using the predicted velocities and compare the resynthesis with the original recording in perceptual loudness space.

The comparison features are Bark-scale specific loudness (BSSL) and Bark-scale total loudness (BSTL), as defined within the Zwicker psychoacoustic framework~\cite{zwicker1999psychoacoustics}. BSSL is a frame-wise critical-band representation, and BSTL is its band-summed contour, tracking perceived loudness over time. Both features capture the main perceptual effect targeted by velocity estimation, namely the temporal shaping of loudness, while reducing sensitivity to timbral and recording-condition mismatch between real audio and SoundFont resynthesis. Two natural alternatives are deliberately avoided. Raw RMS energy ignores frequency-dependent perceptual sensitivity. Note-wise loudness measures, including the PHE and OSF used inside the proxy, are restricted to onset-locked windows and miss the continuous dynamics shape carried by velocity expression. They also coincide with the proxy training signal, which would make the evaluation circular.

Pearson correlation $r$ between real and rendered loudness contours is the primary metric. It is invariant to global scale and offset, so it discounts loudness differences introduced by gain, microphone response, or renderer calibration. Pearson correlation computed on BSSL and BSTL is denoted $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{BSTL}}$, respectively. Cosine similarity ($cs$) and MAE on these features are retained as diagnostic alternatives: cosine similarity is scale-invariant but not offset-invariant, while MAE is sensitive to both. Their behaviour is examined in Section~\ref{sec:bp:discussion}.

Three observations stand out. First, on piano (Table~\ref{tab:bp_results_piano}) the supervised checkpoint remains the strongest reference. Both adaptation strategies underperform on piano because they optimise indirect performance-matching objectives rather than the available velocity labels. This is expected and consistent with the role assigned to the piano experiment: it is a sanity check, not a competition.

Second, on guitar (Table~\ref{tab:bp_results_guitar}) Diff-SFProxy achieves the best $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{BSTL}}$ on both GAPS and FL datasets, outperforming flat velocity, zero-shot transfer, and Diff-Synth across all back-end crop lengths. Diff-Synth, by contrast, does not reliably improve guitar performance and can degrade it. On FL dataset, the $5$\,s Diff-Synth back-end falls below flat velocity in $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ ($0.646$ vs.\ $0.714$). This supports the concern raised in Section~\ref{ssec:bp:adapt_diffsynth}: waveform-level objectives are more exposed to timbral and room-acoustic mismatch than parameter-level supervision.

Third, the asymmetry between the two adaptation interfaces widens with crop length. On FL dataset, Diff-Synth's $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ falls by $0.083$ when moving from $2$\,s to $5$\,s, while Diff-SFProxy's $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ varies within $0.01$. Longer crops give waveform-level objectives more of the underlying timbral mismatch to fit, while parameter-level supervision sees only per-note targets and is therefore much less sensitive to crop length. This mirrors a long-standing observation in inverse-problem design: a supervision signal that only depends on the latent variable of interest is more stable than a high-dimensional signal that mixes that variable with many nuisance factors.

It is also worth noting that the zero-shot piano checkpoint already exceeds flat velocity on both guitar sets, suggesting a partial cross-instrument transfer of velocity sensitivity. Diff-SFProxy adds a consistent further gain on top of this baseline, which is the main practical message of the chapter.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{ssec:bp:ablations}

The bottom rows of Tables~\ref{tab:bp_results_piano} and~\ref{tab:bp_results_guitar} isolate two design choices. Removing $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{anti}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{satu}}$ gives a plausible $\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{Velo}}$ on MAESTRO ($12.9$ vs.\ $10.5$), but $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ falls near the flat-velocity baseline ($0.789$ vs.\ $0.757$). The outputs collapse towards a near-constant value, consistent with the median-seeking behaviour of MAE: central predictions can keep absolute error tolerable while losing the dynamic contour. The two regularisers are therefore not optional. They are required to prevent the model from exploiting the median solution that the loss landscape silently rewards.

Removing the piano-pretrained checkpoint leaves the Diff-SFProxy loss without a supervised velocity anchor. Performance drifts across all datasets and underperforms flat velocity on three of the four guitar metrics. Piano pretraining therefore acts as a crucial prior for cross-instrument adaptation. This is consistent with a more general pattern in transfer learning: a strong supervised initialisation often determines whether a downstream self-supervised objective converges to a useful solution or to a degenerate one.

Figure~\ref{fig:bp_ablations} makes both failure modes visible on a guitar excerpt: without the regularisers (panel c), the predicted velocity contour collapses towards a near-constant value across notes; without MAESTRO pretraining (panel d), the contour drifts away from the reference rather than tracking it. The proposed configuration in panel (b) is the only one of the three that recovers a velocity sequence whose dynamic shape resembles the ground truth in panel (a).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:bp:discussion}

\subsection{What the Recovered Velocity Actually Represents}
\label{ssec:bp:scope}

Velocity is not the only MIDI control that shapes rendered audio loudness. Pedal, reverb, and continuous controllers all contribute in parallel~\cite{ryu2024midfild}. Diff-Synth back-ends such as DDSP-Piano expose several of these controls. Consequently, when adaptation updates only velocity, the velocity gradient can become entangled with residuals caused by fixed or unobserved controls. Diff-SFProxy avoids this contamination by treating velocity as the only loudness-shaping degree of freedom, but the choice carries its own cost. The recovered velocity absorbs effects that, in the real performance, came from other parallel controls.

The recovered velocity is therefore not a canonical property of the acoustic performance. It is defined relative to the chosen SoundFont as the velocity that best reproduces the perceptual dynamics of the original audio. This renderer-conditioned velocity is the practical deployment target for label-scarce expressive analysis or resynthesis, in line with the operational definition adopted in Section~\ref{sec:bp:problem}. Two consequences follow. First, the same real recording can give rise to different recovered velocities under different SoundFonts, so any reported velocity is implicitly tagged by the renderer used. Second, the evaluation metric must be robust to the SoundFont choice and yet sensitive enough to distinguish expressive from flat dynamics. The next subsection tests whether the metric used in this chapter satisfies these two requirements.

To test dynamics sensitivity, $\Delta(\mathrm{GT} - \mathrm{Flat\,64})$ asks whether a metric can distinguish expressive resynthesis from a flat one. Among the similarity metrics, Pearson $r$ produces the larger separation on both BSSL ($+0.118$) and BSTL ($+0.263$). Cosine similarity also orders GT above flat, but it severely compresses the margin ($+0.052$ on BSSL and $+0.047$ on BSTL), proving \emph{insensitive} to fine dynamics differences.

To evaluate renderer robustness, $\Delta\lvert\mathrm{Sal.} - \mathrm{YDP}\rvert$ asks whether a metric is stable when the rendering SoundFont is swapped while the underlying note velocities are kept the same. Here, MAE proves \emph{over-sensitive} to absolute gain: it drifts by $0.193$ on BSSL and $1.723$ on BSTL, substantially more than the corresponding drift on $r$ ($0.034$ and $0.011$). On real recordings, this drift would conflate dynamics differences with renderer choice and make the evaluation unreliable.

Pearson $r$ on BSSL/BSTL therefore provides the right trade-off for this chapter. Cosine similarity and MAE fail on sensitivity and robustness, respectively, and are kept only as diagnostic alternatives. This is also the reason the main results table reports $r_{\mathrm{BSSL}}$ and $r_{\mathrm{BSTL}}$ as the headline metrics for label-free guitar evaluation.

\subsection{When Diff-Synth Should Still Be Preferred}
\label{ssec:bp:when_diffsynth}

Diff-SFProxy is not a universal replacement for Diff-Synth. Three conditions favour the waveform interface. First, the matched differentiable synthesiser must exist for the target instrument and expose a faithful per-note velocity control. Second, its waveform statistics must be acoustically close to the target recordings. Third, the supervision must be allowed to reach beyond velocity and update auxiliary controls such as pedal or articulation. When all three conditions hold, waveform supervision can in principle deliver richer gradients than parameter supervision, and the present chapter does not contest this in general terms.

The claim of this chapter is narrower. In many cross-instrument settings, none of these three conditions holds robustly. A matched Diff-Synth may not exist for the target instrument, may be expensive to retrain at the desired crop length, or may differ from the real recordings in timbre and recording chain. A practical SoundFont, by contrast, is widely available, plug-and-play, and instrument-agnostic at the proxy-design level. In that regime, a note-wise proxy that keeps only the subset of synthesised behaviour most related to velocity can be the more useful engineering choice. This is the regime in which the present chapter is positioned.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{ssec:bp:limitations}

Several boundaries should be kept in mind. First, the recovered velocity is renderer-conditioned (Section~\ref{ssec:bp:scope}). The SoundFont is selected by ear from a small candidate pool. A mismatched SoundFont would produce a teacher signal that the target recordings cannot match, so renderer choice is part of the operational definition rather than an incidental detail.

Second, the experiments focus on piano and guitar. Both instruments are predominantly percussive in their attack profile, which favours the OSF parameter. Sustained-bow instruments such as violin, or breath-driven instruments such as flute, would require acoustic parameters that capture sustain and continuous-modulation cues beyond the PHE/OSF pair used here. The claim is therefore tied to the tested attack-driven setting.

Third, the framework treats velocity as a single one-dimensional control. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:bp:problem}, this is an inherent simplification for many acoustic instruments, where multiple continuous controls shape note dynamics. The recovered velocity is therefore best read as a coarse note-level dynamics coordinate rather than as a complete description of expressive intent.

Fourth, the framework relies on aligned note events. Onset and duration errors propagate into the PHE/OSF windows and into the Diff-Synth rendering, and would degrade adaptation accordingly. The chapter assumes a score-informed setting in which alignment is already good enough for note-wise analysis, keeping the experiment focused on the supervision interface rather than on alignment recovery.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:bp:summary}
This chapter returned to note-level MIDI velocity, but removed the condition that made supervised piano work possible: ground-truth velocity labels in the target domain. The proposed framework treats beyond-piano velocity estimation as a missing-supervision problem. A piano-pretrained VeloEst supplies the prior, while adaptation is driven through a renderer interface. The comparison between Diff-Synth and Diff-SFProxy shows that the useful question is not simply whether the renderer is differentiable, but whether its gradient is concentrated on the control variable being estimated.

Diff-SFProxy is preferred in the tested guitar setting because it supervises velocity through note-wise loudness-related parameters rather than through waveform reconstruction. This reduces the influence of timbre, room, microphone, and renderer mismatch, and makes the recovered velocity a renderer-conditioned dynamics coordinate rather than a claim about the player’s physical gesture. The ablations are important here: without piano pretraining and anti-collapse regularisation, the model can drift toward median or near-constant solutions. The chapter therefore closes the velocity branch of the thesis under the weakest supervision assumption considered here: aligned note events remain available, but the velocity target itself must be constructed through a perceptually grounded proxy.
