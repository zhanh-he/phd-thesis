ABSTRACT
MIDI velocity is crucial for capturing expressive dynam-
ics in human performances. In practical scenarios, a music
score with inaccurate velocities may be available along-
side the performance audio (e.g., music education and
free online archives), enabling the task of score-informed
MIDI velocity estimation. In this work, we propose a
modular, lightweight score-informed Transformer correc-
tion module that refines the velocity estimates of Auto-
matic Music Transcription (AMT) systems. Leveraging
high-quality expressive datasets for rigorous evaluation,
we focus on piano transcription, integrating our module
into multiple existing systems. Trained exclusively on the
MAESTRO dataset, our method consistently reduces ve-
locity errors and improves cross-dataset generalization to
SMD and MAPS datasets. Under this training protocol,
integrating our score-informed module with HPT (named
Score-HPT) establishes a new state-of-the-art, outperform-
ing existing score-informed methods and piano transcrip-
tion models while adding only 1 M parameters. Further-
more, our framework remains robust to temporal misalign-
ments caused by imperfect audio-score synchronization, a
common real-world challenge.

Previous score-informed approaches [14, 15] applied a

scratch. In contrast, we propose a modular approach built

lightweight Transformer encoder as a score-informed cor-
rection module. Inspired by how musicians reference
a music score to make corrections, our design refines
AMT-estimated MIDI velocity with minimal architectural
changes, enabling easy integration into existing systems.
To evaluate its effectiveness, we integrated the proposed
module into several well-known piano transcription mod-
els, including High-Resolution Piano Transcription (HPT)
[16], HPPNet [17], and a recent piano dynamic estima-
tion (DynEst) system [18]. Furthermore, we conducted ro-
bustness evaluations under imperfect audio-score synchro-
nization, demonstrating that our framework remains robust
to temporal misalignments commonly encountered in real-
world scenarios. 1
1. INTRODUCTION
Automatic music transcription (AMT) is a longstanding
topic in Music Information Retrieval (MIR), aiming to cap-
ture symbolic music information from audio. While tradi-
tional AMT systems focus on pitch and note boundaries to
construct the basic MIDI score [1], recent advancements
have expanded to estimating MIDI velocity in piano per-
formances [2]. This expansion has facilitated the creation
of large-scale piano datasets [3–7], benefiting downstream
MIR tasks such as expressive rendering, music education
and generation [8, 9]. Despite recent progress, AMT out-
puts still require post-correction. Prior work has addressed
temporal discrepancies through audio-to-score alignment
[10–12]. By contrast, our work focuses on refining the
MIDI velocity predicted by AMT systems.
Leveraging music score to aid velocity estimation from
audio is known as score-informed MIDI velocity estima-
tion. This paradigm is a practical scenario where a refer-

2.2 Automatic Piano Transcription
Recent piano AMT systems have increasingly been esti-
mating MIDI velocity, thanks to high-quality expressive
piano datasets [28–30]. This progression includes the
Onsets and Frames [31], the T5 Transformer [32], the
HPT system [16], and the Hierarchical Frequency-Time
Transformer (hFT-Transformer) [33]. With a different
focus, Semi-CRF [34] was proposed to refine pitch and
note-boundary estimates, while its recent development us-
ing Transformer (Transkun v2) [35] achieved significant
Notew/Off&Velo scores across many piano datasets, repre-
senting the current SOTA. Meanwhile, other AMT studies
propose competing solutions that prioritize broader gener-
alization [36–38], parameter efficiency [17], or extend the
focus to pedal [39].
Among these systems, HPT serves as a highly prevalent
baseline. It has been used in creating well-known piano
datasets (e.g., EMOPIA [3], GiantMIDI [4], ATEPP [5],
PiJAMA [6], Pianist8 [7]) and has been adapted for gui-
tar transcription [40,41] as well as downstream MIR tasks.
While HPT may not hold the current SOTA for piano tran-
scription accuracy, its lightweight, modular CRNN archi-
tecture ensures computational efficiency and ease of modi-
fication. Similar advantageous qualities are found in HPP-
Net [17] and DynEst [18], making them ideal candidates
for evaluating our proposed approach.

3.4 Post-Processing
Velocity is defined at note onset. It reflects the instanta-
neous intensity of a keystroke rather than a time-varying
loudness curve [19]. This onset-centered definition is con-
sistent with the physical mechanism of the piano. Once a
key has been fully pressed down, subsequent changes in
finger pressure do not affect the tone. In other words, vi-
brato is technically impossible on an acoustic piano.
In our setting, a time-aligned MIDI score is available,
but it does not provide reliable expressive dynamics labels
such as note velocity. Instead, it only provides the ground-
truth note positions, i.e., onsets and frames. We therefore
bypass AMT-style note detection and focus only on veloc-
ity estimation. Consequently, we take the predicted veloc-
ity at each onset and assign this value to the entire note
event, consistent with the MIDI specification.
4. EXPERIMENT
4.1 Dataset
In this study, we used the MAESTRO v3.0.0 dataset [28]
with its default train/validation/test split. This dataset com-
prises 1,276 Yamaha Disklavier piano performances, total-
ing over 200 hours of precisely aligned audio-MIDI data.
A Yamaha Disklavier is an acoustic grand piano with an in-
tegrated electronic system that records MIDI data from hu-
man performance. Thus, MAESTRO provides audio cap-
tured by microphones, reflecting the acoustic environment,
and its tightly synchronized MIDI data.
Following previous studies [14], we trained our model
exclusively on the MAESTRO train set, and evaluated it
on the Saarland Music Data (SMD) dataset [29]. SMD
consists of human performances recorded on a Yamaha
Disklavier piano, but differs from MAESTRO in acoustic
environment and recording conditions. To further assess
cross-dataset generalization, we evaluated our model on 60
piano recordings from the MAPS dataset [30], exposing it
to an additional set of acoustic scenarios.
4.2 Training Setup
For each selected AMT system, we trained two models
in similar configurations. The baseline model is the stan-
dalone velocity branch of the original AMT system. For in-
stance, our HPT baseline utilizes only the velocity branch,
which contains about one-quarter of the full HPT system’s
parameters. Its score-informed counterpart (e.g., Score-
HPT) integrates this same branch with our proposed cor-
rection module. This ensures a fair comparison by isolat-
ing the impact of score information on velocity estimation.

In this work, we presented a novel, modular score-
informed Transformer correction module for refining
MIDI velocity in automatic piano transcription when a ref-
erence score is available. By operating on score onset
tokens and predicting bounded residual updates, the pro-
posed module can be attached to an existing AMT velocity
branch with minimal architectural changes. Experiments
with MAESTRO-only training demonstrate consistent im-
provements across multiple piano transcription systems
(HPT, HPPNet, and DynEst), stronger cross-dataset gen-
eralization to SMD and MAPS, and state-of-the-art per-
formance on SMD under our training protocol, surpass-
ing both prior score-informed models and competitive pi-
ano transcription systems. We further demonstrate that the
proposed framework is robust to audio-score temporal mis-
alignments.
A key limitation of the current study is its restriction to
piano datasets, constrained by the fact that high-fidelity
MIDI velocity ground truth is predominantly captured via
acoustic pianos equipped with velocity sensors. For many
other instruments, velocity annotations are scarce or un-
available, making supervised training and evaluation diffi-
cult. An important direction for future work is to transfer
the proposed framework from piano to other instruments
for MIDI velocity estimation, potentially leveraging self-
supervised learning, transfer learning, or continual learn-
ing to reduce dependence on explicit velocity labels.
