Abstract
Automatic music transcription has matured in its treatment of note pitch and timing, but musical
dynamics remains less fully addressed. This thesis treats dynamics as a missing expressive label
layer that can be recovered from music data already partly specified, without rewriting note
content or note timing. Its unifying question is how musically interpretable dynamics labels
can be recovered from incomplete, weakly expressive, or weakly supervised music data. Two
complementary targets within expressive music performance recur throughout: note-level MIDI
velocity, the per-onset control variable used in MIDI workflows, and score-level dynamics, the
beat-aligned markings such as p, mf, and f written in notation.
The thesis first addresses the symbolic-only setting by reformulating MIDI velocity completion
as image colourisation on sparse piano-roll matrices. A U-Net with window attention and a
sparsity-aware loss recovers the missing velocity layer, and the results are validated through
cross-dataset objective metrics and a MUSHRA-inspired listening test.
It then turns to score-informed velocity estimation in the audio-plus-score setting, using a
Transformer correction module that refines AMT-estimated velocity at aligned score onsets.
Rather than replacing the AMT backbone, the module uses score structure as a late correction
prior, transfers across the HPT, HPPNet, and DynEst backbones, improves cross-dataset
behaviour from MAESTRO to SMD, and remains stable under audio–score timing shifts of up
to several seconds.
A further study treats the audio-only setting with a score-facing output. It jointly estimates
beat-synchronous dynamic markings, change points, beats, and downbeats from performance
audio. A Bark-scale specific loudness front-end, a multi-scale encoder, and an MMoE decoder
are combined to address the conflicting temporal requirements of dynamic-level estimation,
change-point detection, and metrical localisation, producing output that can be written back
into a score.
Finally, the thesis reports an exploratory extension beyond piano. To recover note-level
velocity for instruments without direct labels, it treats the problem as renderer-conditioned
analysis-by-synthesis and compares two differentiable synthesis interfaces. The proposed differ-
entiable SoundFont proxy supervises velocity through note-wise loudness-related descriptors
rather than waveforms. On guitar, this proxy interface adapts a piano-pretrained estimator
more reliably than a differentiable waveform synthesiser under recording mismatch.
Taken together, these studies form a scenario map rather than an end-to-end pipeline. They
show that dynamics recovery becomes tractable when the output layer, temporal coordinate,
evidence source, and supervision regime are matched to one another. The thesis therefore
extends expressive AMT beyond note detection by making existing music data more expressive,
editable, and usable in settings where direct dynamics labels are partial or absent.
Keywords: automatic music transcription; expressive music performance; MIDI velocity;
dynamic markings; score-informed velocity estimation; differentiable SoundFont proxy.

Chapter 1
Introduction
A structurally correct sequence of notes is only the foundation of a musical performance.
When performers receive a composed score, they do not merely execute a rigid sequence of
instructions. They interpret the piece and imbue it with expressiveness. A central dimension of
this interpretation is dynamics. This involves the deliberate shaping of loudness over individual
notes, phrases, and larger formal units. Dynamics conveys musical intent and emotional nuance,
balances musical texture, and is often the clearest signal that distinguishes a convincing human
performance from a flat mechanical rendering.
Music performance is commonly represented in two complementary symbolic forms. The
first is MIDI, where note events are paired with note-level expressive parameters such as velocity.
MIDI velocity is precise, machine-readable, and often comes from the sensor measurements in
musical instruments. The second is the music score, where dynamics is represented by human-
readable markings such as pp, p, mf, f, ff. These markings are relative, context-dependent, and
less precise as physical measurements, but they communicate musical intent on a musically
meaningful metrical grid.
Automatic Music Transcription (AMT) aims to bridge human performance audio and
symbolic music representations, including MIDI and scores. Driven by deep learning and large
aligned datasets [5], recent AMT systems can transcribe note events with high accuracy. Yet
this progress remains asymmetric [6]. The field is proficient at recovering what notes are played
and when they occur. Recovering how loud those notes are played has progressed less, and so
have other expressive dimensions, as depicted in Figure 1.1
This thesis posits that dynamics is a foundational component of expressive AMT. While music
expression is multifaceted, dynamics represents the most perceptually salient and practically
essential dimension. Unlike a standard transcription that yields flat intensities, a dynamics-aware
approach enables expressive rendering and high-quality symbolic corpora curation. By focusing
on the robust recovery of score dynamics and MIDI velocity, this work provides a critical layer
of musical information.

1.1 Motivation
Transcribing correct pitches and timing is only the foundational step of AMT. Incorporating
dynamics into transcription results significantly enhances their capacity to carry expressive
intent, taking the form of MIDI velocity or score dynamics. This is not only a technical
improvement. Transitioning towards such expressive AMT unlocks significant practical benefits,
as outlined below:
MIDI production and expressive rendering. MIDI is the dominant representation in
digital music production, supporting efficient editing and flexible rendering. Yet, much software-
generated MIDI is created via step input and quantisation, leaving expressive parameters at
a lifeless, default constant. Among these, MIDI velocity is one of the most accessible control
variables for shaping note intensity and expressive contour [7, 8]. Creators often want to preserve
their composed notes and timing. To inject human-like expression, they simply need to adjust
velocity values. Still, manual editing is time-consuming and delicate. Developing automated
methods for velocity completion becomes an essential step for realistic rendering.
Music education and performance feedback. Score dynamics is also a core pedagogical
dimension. In automatic piano performance assessment, dynamics is routinely treated alongside
pitch, timing, and rhythm as one of the salient components of performance quality and feedback
[9]. More recent work on query-based music coaching likewise emphasises that useful educational
feedback needs interpretable information about expressive shaping, not only note correctness
[10, 11]. In many educational scenarios, a reference score is already available together with a
student performance audio. This makes score-informed dynamics estimation especially attractive,
because the symbolic prior can stabilise the prediction while keeping the feedback musically
meaningful.
2

=== PDF PAGE 30; LABEL 3 ===
Expressive data curation by enhancing existing archives. A third motivation comes
from data curation. Many large-scale music archives provide aligned audio and symbolic content
(MIDI sequences or digital scores). However, expressive labels are consistently missing. Velocities
are often flattened to a default value, and score dynamic markings are absent or unreliable,
limiting the utility of these corpora for downstream applications. Furthermore, a deep semantic
gap exists between physical machine measurements (MIDI velocity) and subjective human
cognition (score dynamics). Bridging this gap and recovering the missing dimensions is what
turns static archives into expressive datasets.
By contrast, recollecting new datasets from scratch is a costly process constrained by audio-
to-score synchronisation challenges, required musical expertise, and copyright restrictions [12–14].
Therefore, this thesis treats label recovery as a scalable, principled strategy. By retroactively
estimating missing dynamics on existing corpora, we entirely bypass acquisition bottlenecks and
annotation ambiguities, unlocking a vast, previously untapped resource for data-driven music
research.
1.2 Dynamics as a Target in Expressive AMT
Expressive AMT extends beyond note transcription to capture the full aspects of a human
performance. A comprehensive expressive transcription can be conceptually divided into three
layers [15]:
• Timing Layer: Captures the temporal elasticity of a performance, including macro-level
tempo variation, beat-level timing, inter-onset intervals (IOIs), and micro-level articulation.
• Dynamic Layer: Represents the intensity and energy of the music performance, bridging
physical machine measurements (e.g., MIDI velocity) with semantic cognitive notations
(e.g., score dynamic markings).
• Control Layer: Includes instrument-specific techniques and acoustic modifiers, such as
sustain pedalling, vibrato, reverberation, and amplifier configurations.
While all three layers are essential for the computational understanding of expressiveness in
performance, this thesis singles out the Dynamic Layer as its primary focus. This choice does
not diminish the importance of timing or control parameters. Rather, dynamics is chosen to
move AMT beyond notes for three reasons:
Perceptual salience. Dynamics is one of the most immediately audible dimensions of
expressive performance. Listeners often perceive phrase direction, accent, and balance through
intensity change before they perceive subtler aspects such as pedalling or micro-timing [15, 16].
A transcription without dynamics therefore ignores a central part of musical meaning.
3

Symbolic editability. Unlike complex continuous controls (e.g., guitar overdrive or piano
pedalling), dynamics offers an exceptionally tractable and editable symbolic representation.
MIDI velocity is a universal loudness variable for all instruments in digital audio workstations
(DAWs), while score dynamics provide a standardised notational vocabulary for composition,
pedagogy, and performance analysis [15, 17]. This dual status makes dynamics a robust bridge
between low-level MIR outputs and higher-level musical practice.
Data scalability. Many expressive targets, particularly in the Control Layer, depend on
fine-grained, instrument-specific mechanisms. Although recent datasets have begun to capture
control parameters, such parameters remain sparse and weakly standardised [18–22] across
different instruments. By contrast, dynamics has already proved tractable across several practical
paradigms, including piano and singing-voice dynamics estimation [23, 24]. It thus provides a
scalable entry point for expressive AMT, bypassing the bottleneck of instrument-specific data
scarcity.
1.3 Two Complementary Forms of Dynamics
Having established dynamics as our primary expressive target, we clarify its two complementary
symbolic forms within this thesis, as summarised in Table 1.1.
MIDI velocity. Originating from the physical sensors of electronic keyboards and pianos,
MIDI velocity is a machine-centric measurement of physical intensity. To say it operates as a
”note-level” parameter means that every music note from a discrete key strike is assigned an
independent, static integer representing its initial strike force. This strict per-note measurement
dictates that velocity functions as a discrete loudness control parameter. It is highly granular
and absolute within its own scale, yet the resulting loudness highly depends on the acoustic
environment and the specific instrument being played. Because MIDI velocity is necessary for
MIDI-to-audio playback in DAWs and synthesisers, a common default is set to 64 when it is
missing.
Score dynamics. Score dynamics represents the subjective cognitive intention of the composer
or performer. Instead of attaching to isolated notes, markings like p or crescendo are conceptually
anchored to the underlying metrical structure, acting as shared values across a specific beat

1.4 Research Gap and Thesis Stance
This section identifies a narrower and more practical research gap than the general claim that
AMT still lacks expressiveness. A recurring paradox in music research is that while structural data
(pitch and timing) is increasingly abundant, expressive data remains systematically impoverished.
A MIDI file may contain correct note sequences while leaving all velocities at a default constant.
A performance corpus may preserve audio and aligned scores but only sparse, unreliable, or
absent dynamic markings. The common problem is therefore how to recover a missing expressive
label layer from data that are already partly specified.
The ontological gap: dynamics must be inferred across representations. This is not
a simple label-conversion problem. Audio loudness is an acoustic and perceptual phenomenon.
MIDI velocity is a machine-centric, physical parameter. Score dynamics is a cognitive, context-
dependent notation of musical intention. These three forms are related but encode different
concepts. A method that translates one to another must bridge the fundamental gap between
objective physical measurement and subjective human cognition.
The ambiguity of ground truth: dynamics is more fragile than notes. For note
transcription, pitch and onset often remain learnable under moderate changes in instrument,
room, or recording conditions. Dynamics ground truth is less stable. Small changes in instruments
(timbre), microphone placement, editorial convention, or synthesis setup can alter the apparent
relation between sound, MIDI velocity, and score dynamics notation. For non-piano settings,
the difficulty becomes sharper because the bottleneck is not ordinary domain shift but missing
measurement itself: reliable MIDI velocity labels are rarely available, and in some cases even
the ground truth is instrument-dependent.
Fragmented supervision regimes. The MIR community lacks a singular, omnipotent
dataset that pairs audio with both cognitive score annotations and physical velocity labels.
Supervision is deeply fragmented: some corpora provide aligned scores without performance
control, while others provide precise velocity data but no human-readable score. A robust
thesis on expressive dynamics cannot rely on an idealised, fully supervised data regime. It must
systematically address these disjointed data environments.
Thesis stance: label recovery under structural preservation. For these reasons, this
thesis adopts a unified label-recovery perspective. Rather than treating the problem as music

1.5 Unified Thesis Question and Three Core Scenarios
The thesis is driven by the following question:
How can musically interpretable dynamics labels be recovered from
incomplete, weakly expressive, or weakly supervised music data without
rewriting note content or note timing?
This question is studied under three core scenarios, outlined in Table 1.2. These scenarios

2.2 Dynamics in audio
In audio, musical dynamics are most naturally discussed through loudness, a psychoacoustic
quantity. It is shaped not only by physical intensity or sound-pressure level but also by frequency-
dependent hearing sensitivity, critical-band interaction, masking, and temporal integration [28].
This distinction matters throughout the thesis because the audio layer must later be related
to MIDI and score. A frame with greater broadband energy is not necessarily perceived as
dynamically stronger if the change is mainly timbral or spectrally redistributed.
For this reason, dynamics analysis in audio benefits from perceptually informed input features.
Loudness-inspired features aim to approximate human intensity perception more directly than
generic spectral magnitudes or raw energy measures. Recent singing-voice studies show that
loudness-oriented and Bark-based representations are competitive with, and in some settings
superior to, conventional log-Mel features for dynamics-related estimation tasks [24, 33]. This
motivates the Bark-scale representation used later in the audio-only score dynamics component
of this thesis.

2.2.4 Other Audio Representations Related to Loudness
Besides explicit loudness measures, MIR also relies on time–frequency representations that retain
amplitude information with different degrees of perceptual relevance. At the simplest level,
short-time RMS energy provides a frame-level estimate of signal power and is widely used as a
baseline descriptor of local intensity. However, RMS remains only a coarse proxy for perceived
loudness: it reduces each analysis frame to a single scalar and therefore cannot represent how
dynamic impression also depends on the spectral distribution of energy, rather than on overall
level alone. This limitation is important because perceived dynamics are not determined by
level alone. The pitch, loudness, and timbre can jointly affect perceived instrument dynamics,
and broader MIR work on musical affect and tension also treats loudness as only one cue among
several acoustic factors [43–45].
For this reason, sub-band spectral representations are often more informative than scalar
energy features for dynamics-related MIR tasks, especially the Mel-based representations [43, 46–
48]. A Mel spectrogram aggregates spectral energy into perceptually motivated frequency bands,
preserving not only how much energy is present but also where that energy lies in the spectrum
[49]. A log-Mel spectrogram further applies logarithmic compression, reducing the dominance of
very large amplitudes, stabilising the representation across a wide dynamic range, and making
it more consistent with the nonlinear character of loudness perception. Nevertheless, log-Mel
does not explicitly model human loudness perception. Consequently, recent works in loudness
estimation typically employ log-Mel as a standard baseline against which the superior loudness
modelling capabilities of BSSL are compared [24, 26].
Compact cepstral features such as MFCCs and BFCCs provide lower-dimensional summaries
of filterbank energies on perceptual frequency scales. MFCCs are derived from Mel-scale
filterbank energies and have long been used in speech and music modelling [50, 51], while BFCCs
apply the same general cepstral idea on the Bark scale [52]. These features are attractive when
compactness, decorrelation, and robust timbral description are important, and they remain
common in MIR applications such as music classification and instrument recognition [43, 46, 53].
However, this compression is also their main limitation for fine-grained dynamics analysis: by
transforming sub-band energies into a compact cepstral representation, they remove part of
the band-wise structure that may carry local loudness, resonance, and spectral-balance cues.
BFCCs inherit the same trade-off on the Bark scale. For dynamics-focused modelling, directly
retaining Mel/Bark band energies or using specific-loudness representations is therefore usually
easier to justify than relying on cepstral summaries alone [24, 26, 48, 54].
By contrast, the Constant-Q Transform (CQT) is designed around logarithmically spaced
frequency bins whose spacing aligns closely with musical pitch [55]. This makes CQT well
suited to pitch-, harmony-, and transcription-oriented tasks, where the representation benefits
from a frequency axis organised according to musical scale structure [48, 56]. CQT can still

2.3 Dynamics in MIDI and Score
Audio captures the sounding result of dynamics: pressure variation, spectral energy, and
perceived loudness. Symbolic formats describe dynamics through a different kind of abstraction.
In a score, dynamics are written as dynamic markings: sparse, human-readable instructions
such as p, f, and crescendo. In MIDI, dynamics are represented primarily by velocity: a dense,
machine-readable control value attached to individual note onsets. These two representations
are connected through performance, but they are not interchangeable measurements. Dynamic
markings describe an expressive intention over musical context; MIDI velocity records or controls
local note attack. The gap between them is therefore a central modelling problem rather than a
formatting problem.
2.3.1 Dynamic markings as macro-level musical instructions
Dynamic markings are the conventional score vocabulary for notated loudness and dynamic
change. They include static markings such as pp, p, mp, mf, f, and ff, as well as directional
indications such as crescendo, diminuendo, and hairpins. Although a marking is printed at a
specific visual position, its musical scope is usually broader than that position. A single p may
govern a phrase, a texture, or a formal region until a later marking or musical event changes the
dynamic state. A crescendo indicates a tendency over a span, not an isolated value at one note.
The crucial point is that dynamic markings are not calibrated volume labels. They are
relational instructions. A forte passage is not defined by a fixed sound-pressure level, nor does a
piano passage prescribe a fixed physical intensity. The intended realisation depends on register,
texture, melodic role, harmonic tension, instrumentation, acoustic setting, historical style, and
the performer’s interpretive priorities. A melody marked p may require more acoustic energy
than an accompaniment marked mf ; an inner voice may remain physically soft while sounding
perceptually salient because of register or timbral contrast. This context dependence is central
to empirical work on the relation between dynamic markings and performed loudness [17, 30].
Dynamic markings are therefore macro-level representations. They compress many local
performance decisions into a small number of expressive symbols. Their value lies precisely
in this compression: they allow a composer or editor to specify the character of a passage
without prescribing every note attack. Expressive performance research treats dynamics as one
dimension of a broader transformation from score structure into timing, intensity, articulation,
and timbral choices [15, 57]. Recent modelling work on piano performance also shows that
performers produce systematic dynamic profiles from phrase structure, metrical position, register,
and style, even when explicit dynamic markings are removed [58]. Dynamic markings should
18

=== PDF PAGE 46; LABEL 19 ===
therefore be understood as musically precise but physically under-specified instructions.
For MIR, this has a direct consequence. A dynamic marking is a weak but meaningful label.
It is weak because it does not determine exact amplitude, MIDI velocity, or loudness. It is
meaningful because it identifies the expressive region that a listener, performer, or teacher can
recognise in the score. In this thesis, the term dynamic markings is used for this score-facing
representation throughout, and its practical coordinate is the beat, bar, or phrase rather than
the audio frame.
2.3.2 MIDI velocity as micro-level performance control
MIDI velocity is a different kind of representation. It is attached to a note onset rather than
to a phrase or region. In conventional MIDI performance data, a sounding note onset carries
a discrete velocity value on a finite integer scale. In a chord, each note may have a different
velocity. In a rapid passage, every note can carry its own local attack value. MIDI velocity is
therefore a micro-level representation: it serialises note-level actions rather than summarising
the expressive state of a passage.
The word velocity is often described informally as key-strike force. For keyboard instruments
this is useful but imprecise. In sensor-equipped acoustic pianos, the recorded value is more
accurately treated as a discretised proxy for key or hammer motion near the note attack. Studies
of computer-controlled pianos distinguish the player’s physical action, the hammer–string
interaction, the measured control value, and the resulting acoustic signal [16, 59]. Large piano
datasets such as MAESTRO use this property to provide aligned audio and MIDI data with
key-strike velocity labels, making note-level velocity a practical target for MIR systems [5].
MIDI velocity is exact as symbolic data. A note with velocity 80 remains velocity 80 when
the MIDI file is copied, edited, or passed to a model. It is also mechanical in the sense that it
refers to a local performance control: how strongly a note attack is executed or how strongly
a synthesiser should respond. This objectivity is one reason why velocity is attractive for
automatic transcription, expressive rendering, and symbolic performance modelling [23, 60, 61].
However, exactness in the data structure does not imply universality in sound. MIDI velocity
is not loudness, not amplitude, and not a dynamic marking. It is a control parameter whose
acoustic effect depends on the instrument, renderer, register, sample layer, synthesis design, and
recording chain. Dannenberg’s analysis of MIDI velocity shows that different synthesisers and
patches implement substantially different velocity-response curves [7]. Thus, a MIDI value can
be objective without being perceptually absolute. It is exact as a note-level control value, but
relative as an acoustic and musical quantity.
2.3.3 Bridging the gap between dynamics representations
A tempting solution is to convert between MIDI velocity and dynamic markings with a fixed
lookup table. Such a table might assign low velocities to p, middle velocities to mf, and high
velocities to f ; the inverse table would assign each printed marking a nominal playback velocity.

The failure of this assumption has two main causes. Both follow from the same repre-

coordinates. A velocity value is attached to a note onset, a loudness impression is distributed over
time and frequency, and a score marking describes a contextual musical state. Any direct lookup
table therefore discards the intermediate stages that make dynamics musically meaningful.
Instrument dependence. MIDI velocity is physically arbitrary across instruments. The same
velocity value may trigger different amplitudes, spectra, sample layers, envelopes, or synthesis
behaviours depending on the instrument. Velocity 80 on a sampled grand piano may produce a
bright attack and strong hammer noise; velocity 80 on a soft synthesiser patch may mainly open
a filter or change an envelope; velocity 80 on another renderer may produce only a small loudness
change. Even within piano, the relationship between velocity and acoustic output depends on
pitch, register, hammer mechanics, resonance, microphone placement, and the reproduction
system [7, 16]. A fixed velocity threshold therefore cannot define an instrument-independent
boundary between mp, mf, and f.
This problem also affects the reverse direction. Assigning f to a single nominal velocity may
be adequate for rough playback, but it ignores the instrument-specific response needed to make
20

=== PDF PAGE 48; LABEL 21 ===
a passage sound forte-like. A convincing forte on one instrument may require a different velocity
range, spectral balance, or articulation strategy on another. Rule-based approaches to musical
dynamics can impose useful rendering conventions, but they cannot remove the instrument
dependence of the underlying control-to-sound mapping [66].
Perceptual loudness and performer style. The second obstacle is perceptual and musical.
Human listeners do not hear MIDI velocity values. They hear loudness, salience, balance,
and expressive shape. Loudness perception is nonlinear and depends on frequency-dependent
sensitivity, critical-band interaction, masking, and temporal integration [28, 29]. A dense chord
played with moderate note velocities can be perceived as louder than a single note with a higher
velocity because total loudness depends on how energy is distributed across frequency bands
and time. Conversely, a high-velocity note may be masked by surrounding texture or may fail
to dominate if it occupies a less perceptually prominent register.
Performer style adds another layer. A pianist’s realisation of forte is shaped by phrase
direction, metrical hierarchy, historical idiom, local texture, and personal expressive strategy.
Empirical studies of dynamic markings and performed loudness show that the mapping between
notated symbols and realised loudness is learnable within constrained contexts but unstable
across pieces, performances, and performers [30]. Related modelling work shows that different
pianists can agree on broad dynamic tendencies while still differing in the local strategies used
to produce them [58]. A dynamic marking is therefore not a target number; it is an interpretive
category.
The bridge between MIDI velocity and dynamic markings must consequently model context.
It must account for the instrument that turns velocity into sound, the auditory system that turns
sound into perceived loudness, and the performer or annotator who turns musical context into
an expressive label. A lookup table collapses these stages into one scalar threshold. The later
chapters of this thesis instead treat symbolic dynamics recovery as a structured MIR problem:
note-level velocity estimation, score-informed correction, and beat-synchronous dynamic-marking
recovery address different parts of the same many-to-many relationship between machine-readable
performance control and human-readable musical notation.

2.4.1 Performance research as a precursor to expressive MIR
Music performance research is the historical reason why dynamics cannot be treated as a small
extension to note transcription. Earlier studies of expressive performance showed that timing
and dynamics are shaped by phrase structure, metre, accent, local tension, and instrumental
constraints [15, 16]. These findings matter for MIR because they make dynamics different from
pitch or onset. A pitch label can often be judged against a relatively stable musical event,
whereas a dynamics label depends on how a performer interprets that event in context.
This does not mean that expressive MIR should abandon discrete labels. It means that
the label must be tied to the right musical coordinate. A note-level velocity is meaningful
when the task is to control the rendering of individual note events. A score-level dynamic
marking is meaningful when the task is to annotate a metrical or phrase-level change in
loudness. A frame-level loudness contour is useful as acoustic evidence, but it is not itself a
score annotation. Performance research therefore supplies more than motivation: it explains
why representation choice is a validity condition. A system can be accurate with respect to one
dynamics representation while being musically inappropriate for another.
2.4.2 From note transcription to expressive transcription
Classical AMT focused on recovering pitch content and note boundaries. Recent deep-learning
systems have made this problem increasingly reliable for piano, especially on datasets such as
MAESTRO and MAPS [4, 6, 67–71]. Table 2.5 should therefore be read not only as a benchmark
summary, but as evidence of a shift in the bottleneck. For piano, note and note-plus-offset
scores are now strong enough that the remaining expressive layer becomes visible: velocity-aware
performance remains weaker, and the gap is not simply a matter of detecting more notes. It
reflects the difficulty of estimating how strongly each known note should be rendered.
The multi-instrument case exposes a second bottleneck. On MusicNet and related corpora,
the transcription problem is harder, and velocity-aware evaluation is often absent because
reliable note-level velocity supervision is not available outside sensor-equipped piano datasets.
This creates an asymmetry that runs through the thesis: piano provides the cleanest supervised
setting for velocity recovery, while other instruments often provide audio and aligned notes but
no trustworthy velocity labels. Expressive transcription is therefore not a detached field from
AMT. It is the next layer of symbolic recovery once note content and timing are sufficiently
constrained, and it requires different assumptions about supervision and evaluation.
Expressive AMT is therefore not a separate field detached from AMT. It is the next layer of
symbolic recovery once note content and timing become reasonably stable. The thesis positions
its contributions as extensions of AMT towards expressive and editable symbolic outputs rather
than as alternatives to note transcription.

2.4.3 Three task lines of dynamics-related MIR
The literature separates most clearly not by model family, but by what musical structure is
assumed to be trustworthy. This is the organising principle used in the rest of the chapter.
Note-level MIDI velocity recovery. The first task line assumes that the note events are
already known and asks only for the missing per-note velocity values. This setting is attractive
because it preserves the composition exactly: pitch, onset, and duration remain fixed, while
the model restores an editable control layer. Its limitation is equally clear. MIDI velocity is a
renderer-facing parameter, not a direct measurement of perceived loudness or score dynamics.
It is therefore well suited for expressive rendering and MIDI data curation, but it should not be
treated as a universal representation of musical dynamics.
Beat-synchronous score-dynamics recovery. The second task line aims at dynamic
markings such as p, mf, and f. Here the output is not a velocity value attached to every note,
but a sparse symbolic description aligned with the metrical structure of the score. This changes
both the temporal unit and the semantics of the target. A score marking is intentionally coarse
and contextual; it describes a region of musical interpretation rather than the intensity of a
23

=== PDF PAGE 51; LABEL 24 ===
2. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
single event. Methods in this line must therefore model loudness evidence together with beats,
downbeats, and change points, rather than treating dynamics as frame-wise audio classification
alone.
Renderer-based and proxy-based recovery. The third task line appears when the target-
domain velocity label is missing. In this case, supervised learning from ground-truth velocities is
not available, even if audio and aligned notes exist. A renderer or differentiable proxy can then
provide an indirect training signal: predicted velocities are judged by whether their rendered
dynamics match the performance audio. This formulation is weaker than direct supervision,
but it is not merely a workaround. It changes the claim being made. The recovered value is
a usable dynamics coordinate for rendering or analysis under a specified synthesis condition,
rather than a canonical physical measurement of the performer’s gesture.
2.4.4 Cross-cutting validity: temporal units, alignment, and priors
Independently of the specific task line, three cross-cutting concerns determine whether a
dynamics-related MIR system is even well-posed: which temporal unit indexes the prediction,
whether the supervision is aligned, and what structural prior makes an underdetermined target
tractable.
Temporal units. A dynamics-related event can be represented at several temporal resolutions,
and each resolution carries a different modelling assumption. At the densest level, log-Mel
and BSSL provide frame-level time–frequency evidence, while BSTL collapses the Bark-wise
loudness distribution into a frame-level loudness trajectory. At the symbolic level, note onsets
and note events index the local control values used for MIDI velocity. At the score-facing level,
beats, downbeats, and phrase boundaries provide the coordinates on which dynamic markings,
change points, and longer expressive spans become musically interpretable. Figure 2.3 illustrates
these representations on the same performance excerpt, and Table 2.6 summarises their roles
across the thesis. Choosing the wrong coordinate is not a presentation issue. It changes what
the model can learn and how the prediction should be evaluated.
Alignment. Alignment is not a preprocessing detail. It determines what the supervision
actually means. For note-level velocity, note onsets must be aligned with either the audio or the
reference score; instrumented piano corpora supply this directly through synchronised audio–
MIDI capture [5]. For score dynamics, the critical coordinate is the beat grid: dynamic symbols
are rarely meaningful as an unconstrained frame-wise label stream but become interpretable once
mapped to beats, bars, and phrase-level change points. Figure 2.3 makes this distinction visible:
the same audio frames support several possible symbolic coordinates, but only some of those
coordinates match the intended target. For label-scarce settings, alignment also determines
whether a proxy supervision signal (e.g. rendered audio reconstruction) can be compared to the
observed performance at all.

2.5 Note-Level MIDI Velocity Recovery
This section reviews the first of the three task lines: recovering note-level MIDI velocity
from symbolic notes, audio, or both. It tracks the literature from rule-based humanisation
through symbolic completion, audio-driven AMT, and score-informed correction, and ends at
the boundary where reliable velocity supervision disappears outside piano. Chapters 3 and 4
contribute to this line; the label-scarce extension is deferred to Section 2.7.
2.5.1 Expressive rendering and humanisation
Expressive performance rendering aims to generate expressive control signals from symbolic
input. Many systems predict timing, dynamics, and sometimes articulation jointly [79, 80].
These models are important because they show that expressive performance can be learned
from symbolic structure. At the same time, they reveal a practical limitation for the present
thesis. Joint expressive rendering may alter more than the user wants. In production or corpus-
upgrading settings, the desired operation is often narrower: preserve the notes and timing, and
recover only the missing dynamics layer. This narrower, controllable objective is the entry point
26

=== PDF PAGE 54; LABEL 27 ===
for Chapter 3.
2.5.2 Symbolic velocity completion and prediction
Symbolic velocity completion assumes that the note sequence is already known and that the
missing variable is note-level velocity. Early work used rule-based or statistical approaches
[66]. More recent work formulates the task with deep learning, including convolutional autoen-
coders and sequence-to-sequence models with attention [81, 82]. Diffusion-based note-intensity
estimation has also been explored in related score-informed settings [83].
A central challenge in this literature is the sparsity of symbolic piano-roll data. Dense
frame grids contain large silent regions, and a model that treats all cells equally can waste
much of its capacity. Two architectural patterns recur in response. The first rasterises MIDI as
piano-roll-like matrices indexed by pitch and time [25], then processes them with U-Net-style
encoders that preserve fine local detail through skip connections while aggregating larger context
through the encoder–decoder path. The second couples this locality with windowed or linear
attention to extend the receptive field without the full cost of global attention [84, 85]. Together
they motivate the image-like, sparse-2D formulation adopted in Chapter 3.
2.5.3 Audio-driven velocity estimation within AMT
Velocity recovery from audio has become increasingly feasible for piano because large aligned
datasets exist with sensor-recorded note velocities [5]. Representative velocity-enabled AMT
systems include OaF, HPT, hFT-Trans, and event-based models [4, 67–71]. Related work
also studies robustness, semi-supervised learning, and parameter-efficient piano transcription
[74, 86–89].
Despite strong progress, note-level velocity remains more fragile than note pitch and onset
estimation. The trend is already visible in Table 2.5: as the evaluation tightens from frame F1
to note+offset+velocity F1, the gap between leading systems widens. Domain shift in piano
timbre, microphone placement, room acoustics, and recording chain degrades the mapping
from acoustic evidence to velocity more severely than the mapping from acoustic evidence to
pitch. This fragility is one of the main motivations for the score-informed correction strategy of
Chapter 4.
2.5.4 Score-informed MIDI velocity estimation and correction
Score-informed velocity estimation assumes that an aligned score is available together with
performance audio. Historically, this line of work developed partly because early AMT systems
focused on notes and offered limited support for reliable velocity estimation [90]. Early systems
used hand-designed measurements or parametric estimation strategies [7, 16, 60, 91]. Later work
introduced restricted Boltzmann machines, score-informed non-negative matrix factorisation,
diffusion-based note-level estimation, and FiLM-conditioned deep models [23, 61, 83, 92–95].

A key pattern in this literature is feature fusion. Score information is injected into the
acoustic model during feature extraction. This can improve accuracy, but it also ties the
score-informed method tightly to a particular backbone. The present thesis instead emphasises
late correction. A correction module can attach to an existing AMT system and refine its
velocity estimate while leaving the backbone largely unchanged. The natural interface for this
stance is a note-token representation rather than a dense frame grid: when note events are
already known, focusing modelling capacity on the events that actually require correction is
more efficient than re-running a full transcription model end to end. This is the design stance
developed in Chapter 4, where a score-informed Transformer over note tokens corrects a coarse
velocity estimate from a frozen AMT front-end.
2.5.5 Piano bias and the beyond-piano boundary
Reliable velocity supervision is still strongly piano-centric. Instrumented pianos make note-level
velocity available at scale, but comparable datasets for other instruments are scarce. This creates
both a training bias and an evaluation bias. Transfer beyond piano must therefore confront
two bottlenecks: missing labels and instrument-dependent mappings between performer action,
timbre, and loudness.
The important point is that beyond-piano failure is not only ordinary domain shift. It is a
measurement problem. Outside piano, even defining what a “ground-truth” note-level control
signal should be is non-trivial: there is no sensor analogue of the Disklavier hammer trace for
guitar, violin, or wind instruments. This distinction is essential for the honest positioning of
Chapter 6, and it motivates the analysis-by-synthesis route reviewed in Section 2.7.
2.6 Beat-Synchronous Score-Dynamics Recovery
This section reviews the second task line: recovering beat-anchored dynamic markings such as p,
f, and crescendo from performance audio, together with the metrical structure on which those
markings live. The output is a sparse, score-facing sequence rather than dense note-level control,
and the target representation is therefore quite different from the velocity recovery reviewed in
Section 2.5. Chapter 5 contributes to this task line.
2.6.1 From performed loudness to score dynamics
Computational modelling of dynamics has a long history in expressive performance research
[15]. Rather than focusing only on note-level control, this line of work studies how performed
loudness relates to symbolic dynamic markings and phrase structure. Kosta and collaborators
showed both the usefulness and the ambiguity of this relationship [17, 30, 96]. Other work has
further analysed dynamic principles in piano performance [58].
From a machine-learning perspective, score-dynamics recovery can be framed as classification
or structured sequence labelling. The input is audio. The output is a sequence of dynamic
28

=== PDF PAGE 56; LABEL 29 ===
symbols or dynamic regimes. The choice of audio representation is therefore not neutral. In
the MazurkaBL line of work, the performed-loudness side of this mapping is extracted through
sone-based psychoacoustic processing rather than raw amplitude, precisely because the target
concerns perceived intensity rather than unweighted energy [97]. More generally, perceptual
front-ends such as Bark-scale specific loudness preserve both a scalar loudness trajectory and
the Bark-wise distribution of loudness across the spectrum, which gives a better inductive
bias for loudness-oriented tasks than log-Mel features [24, 26]. Figure 2.3(a)–(c) shows this
contrast on the same excerpt. The practical consequence is also clear: a Nyquist-constrained
Bark front-end is substantially lower-dimensional than a 128-band log-Mel representation, which
enables longer audio segments and the phrase-level context that score dynamics actually requires.
The challenge is that the labels themselves are sparse, relative, and musically contextual rather
than physically absolute. This is one reason why a beat-synchronous formulation is more natural
than direct frame-wise regression of continuous loudness.
2.6.2 Change points and dynamic segmentation
Dynamic markings rarely behave as independent frame labels. They form local regimes that
persist over spans of music, and musical meaning often lies in the transitions between those
regimes. Change-point detection therefore plays an important role. It supports segmentation,
interpretation, and the recovery of phrase-level dynamic structure [96, 97]. The phrase markers
in Figure 2.3(g) show the kind of longer span that cannot be recovered from isolated note or
frame evidence alone. In Chapter 5, change points are estimated jointly with dynamic levels so
that a score-facing output includes both local states and their transitions.
2.6.3 Beat and downbeat tracking as structural coordinates
Beat and downbeat tracking has a mature literature. Early deep-learning approaches treated
beats and downbeats as jointly modelled targets using recurrent neural networks combined
with probabilistic post-processing such as dynamic Bayesian networks [98], an RNN–DBN
paradigm that dominated the field for several years. Subsequent work refined this combination
with stronger convolutional encoders and tighter post-processing [99, 100], while more recent
Transformer-based systems further reduce reliance on heavy post-processing [101]. For this
thesis, beat and downbeat tracking is not only a useful auxiliary task. It is the coordinate
system that makes score dynamics interpretable, comparable, and writable back to a score, as
reflected in the bottom grid of Figure 2.3.
2.6.4 Multi-scale and multi-task formulations
Dynamics estimation and metrical estimation are closely related but not identical, and they
live at different temporal scales. Beat and downbeat tracking needs fine temporal resolution
to localise peaks; phrase-level dynamics needs much longer context to read shape, contrast,

and change-point structure. Multi-scale architectures address this scale mismatch by providing
parallel or hierarchical pathways with different effective receptive fields [102].
The remaining issue is that even a shared multi-scale encoder can suffer from negative transfer
when several tasks compete for the same representation. Multi-task learning is attractive when
the outputs share information, but it is dangerous when the tasks prefer different acoustic cues.
Mixture-of-experts methods provide a practical compromise: they combine shared representation
learning with task-specific routing so that each output draws differently from a shared expert
pool [27]. The MMoE-style decoder used in Chapter 5 follows precisely this logic, and the
combination of a perceptually grounded multi-scale encoder with task-aware routing is the
architectural shape that this task line ultimately motivates.
2.7 Audio Rendering and Differentiable Synthesis
This section reviews the third task line: mapping symbolic control values back to audio through
a renderer, and using that mapping as supervision when direct labels are missing. The review
proceeds in three steps. Section 2.7.1 surveys traditional renderers and the role velocity plays
inside them. Section 2.7.2 reviews differentiable synthesis and the design space it opens.
Section 2.7.3 discusses the analysis-by-synthesis paradigm and its application to label-scarce
velocity recovery in Chapter 6.
2.7.1 Traditional renderers and the velocity control point
Traditional MIDI-to-audio rendering covers three broad families. Sample-based synthesisers such
as SoundFont-driven engines (e.g. FluidSynth [103]) produce sound by selecting prerecorded
samples and post-processing them with envelopes and filters; commercial samplers and free
SoundFonts such as Salamander Grand Piano [104] share this design. Physical models such as
Pianoteq [105] replace samples with mechanical simulations of the instrument. Wavetable, FM,
and additive engines synthesise the signal from compact spectral or oscillator parameters. The
three families differ in the underlying model, but they all expose MIDI velocity as a control
input that drives intensity, timbral brightness, attack character, and sometimes sample-layer
selection.
The implication for dynamics recovery is twofold. First, velocity is not a renderer-independent
loudness unit: the same MIDI velocity can produce substantially different acoustic outputs
depending on which sample layers, envelopes, or physical parameters the renderer uses, as
already discussed in Section 2.3. Figures 2.4 and 2.5 make this concrete by showing how the
velocity-to-loudness response curves of representative SoundFonts differ within the piano family
and across instruments. Second, traditional renderers are not differentiable: they expose discrete
sample selection, table lookups, and non-smooth envelopes, so gradients cannot flow from
rendered audio back to the velocity input. Any system that wants to use rendering as a learning
signal must therefore replace, approximate, or wrap the renderer with a differentiable proxy.

2.7.2 Differentiable synthesis
Differentiable synthesis was introduced to make audio rendering compatible with gradient-
based learning. The DDSP framework expresses a renderer as a small differentiable graph
(harmonic-plus-noise oscillators, time-varying filters) whose control signals are predicted by a
neural network [106, 107]. Loss functions are then applied in the signal or spectral domain, and
gradients flow back through the renderer to the network. The same idea has been adapted to
specific instruments and rendering goals: MIDI-DDSP couples DDSP with explicit note-level
control for expressive performance synthesis [108], DDSP-Piano specialises the framework to
piano timbre with mechanically motivated components [109], and follow-up work extends it to
acoustic guitar and other plucked-string instruments [110, 111]. Beyond DDSP, differentiable
approximations to traditional samplers and effects processors have been studied as well [112–114],
and recent work has explored normalising-flow and transformer-based generative renderers in
similar settings [115, 116].
For dynamics recovery, the appeal is direct. A differentiable renderer turns the velocity-
to-audio mapping into a learnable layer. A model can predict velocity, render the resulting
audio, and back-propagate a reconstruction error. This makes velocity-relevant supervision
possible even when direct note-level labels are absent. The cost is also direct: differentiable
renderers are not free reproductions of every commercial synthesiser. They tend to specialise to
one instrument family, and their fidelity to real recordings depends on how much of the timbral,
recording-chain, and room behaviour the renderer captures. The choice of differentiable renderer
therefore conditions what the downstream model can plausibly learn.
2.7.3 Analysis-by-synthesis and label-scarce velocity recovery
The combination of differentiable rendering and prediction networks defines an analysis-by-
synthesis setting. A network proposes control values for an observed recording; the renderer
turns those values back into audio (or into an intermediate audio-derived quantity); a loss
compares the rendered output with the observed signal. This paradigm becomes the central
tool when true note-level velocity labels are unavailable, because the renderer takes the place of
a label by linking predicted control to a measurable property of the recording.
Two design axes matter for this thesis. The first is the comparison space. Signal-domain
losses (waveform or spectral) can be applied directly to the rendered output, but they conflate
velocity with timbre, recording chain, and room response, so the gradient signal is dominated
by factors unrelated to velocity. Parameter-domain or perceptual proxy losses instead extract a
small set of velocity-relevant acoustic descriptors (e.g. note-wise loudness, attack envelope) from
both the rendered and the observed audio and compare them in that lower-dimensional space.
The latter is closer in spirit to the parameter-extraction proxies used in spectral-modelling
research and trades waveform fidelity for stronger alignment with the velocity target. The
second axis is the renderer’s domain coverage. A differentiable renderer adapted to piano need
not transfer to guitar or violin without further work [109, 110], which is one of the practical

For expressive AMT, this strategy is attractive but delicate. It makes label-scarce learning

Chapter 6 adopts the analysis-by-synthesis route explicitly under these constraints. It contrasts

2.8.1 Dataset modalities and what each chapter can learn
The thesis relies on datasets with very different modality combinations. Audio–MIDI datasets

stable redistribution conditions.

be made at all. Chapter 3 needs reliable symbolic velocity targets. Chapter 4 additionally needs
an aligned score. Chapter 5 needs score-facing dynamics with metrical structure. Chapter 6
needs candidate non-piano corpora, but cannot assume direct velocity supervision of MAESTRO
quality.

2.8.3 Evaluation for score dynamics
Score dynamics is usually evaluated as classification on a musically meaningful grid. F1 score is
common because the class distribution is imbalanced and because some symbols occur much
less frequently than others [97]. In the present thesis, beat-synchronous evaluation is especially
important because it avoids arbitrary frame-level tolerance choices and aligns the prediction
target with how the annotations are actually used in music notation.
Evaluation remains inherently limited by label ambiguity. Different editors or annotators
may disagree on dynamic markings. Different editions of the same piece may contain different
expressive symbols. This does not invalidate the task, but it does mean that score-dynamics
performance must be interpreted with awareness of the annotation convention behind the
dataset.
2.8.4 Evaluation under proxy supervision
When direct note-level velocity labels are absent, evaluation becomes weaker and must be stated
more carefully. Audio reconstruction error after differentiable synthesis is informative, but it is
not equivalent to accurate velocity recovery. A proxy-supervised study should therefore separate
three questions. Does the synthesised audio become more plausible? Do the predicted control
values display musically reasonable distributions and relative trends? Does any anchor subset
with stronger labels show positive transfer?
This evaluation logic is directly relevant to Chapter 6. The contribution of that chapter
is exploratory because the supervision and evaluation are weaker than in the piano chapters.
Making that weakness explicit strengthens the thesis. It does not weaken it.
2.8.5 Reproducibility, alignment quality, and licensing
Expressive AMT research is strongly constrained by data quality and licensing. Many scores are
copyrighted. Many recordings cannot be redistributed freely. Some datasets rely on external
URLs or derived alignments whose long-term availability is uncertain. Alignment quality is
another major factor. A method may fail not because the dynamics model is poor, but because
the score or beat alignment is noisy.
For this reason, reproducible expressive AMT research should document preprocessing,
alignment assumptions, train/validation/test protocols, and all modality constraints clearly.

2.9 Summary of Gaps and Thesis Requirements
The review above leaves six requirements for a thesis on dynamics recovery.
• R1 Preserve note content and timing while recovering dynamics. Many expressive
systems modify several performance dimensions jointly. The present thesis instead treats
dynamics as a refinement or annotation layer. This requirement is most visible in
Chapters 3, 4, and 6.
• R2 Support heterogeneous data-availability settings. Real corpora differ widely in
what is available: symbolic MIDI only, audio plus aligned score, audio only, or audio with
only proxy supervision. This requirement motivates the overall design of Chapters 3–6.
• R3 Respect representation differences explicitly. Loudness, MIDI velocity, and
score dynamics are linked but not identical. The target representation must therefore
be chosen deliberately and defended in each chapter. This requirement spans the whole
thesis.
• R4 Use musically meaningful coordinates for score-level outputs. Score dynamics
should not be treated as an arbitrary frame-wise label stream. It should be expressed
on a beat-synchronous interface, supported by downbeat and phrase-level context, that
supports interpretation, evaluation, and symbolic writing. This requirement motivates
Chapter 5.
• R5 Evaluate with both numerical and musical evidence. Objective metrics are
necessary but not always sufficient. Listening tests, qualitative inspection, distributional
behaviour, and cross-dataset evaluation remain important. This requirement is visible in
Chapters 3, 5, and 6.
• R6 Treat beyond-piano transfer as a supervision problem rather than ordinary
domain shift. Once true note-level velocity labels disappear, the central issue is no longer
only acoustic domain mismatch, but the absence of a directly measurable target. This
requirement motivates Chapter 6, where proxy supervision is formulated as a renderer-
conditioned route to label-scarce velocity recovery.
These requirements define the dissertation stance. Dynamics recovery is treated as controlled
label restoration under structural preservation, not as unconstrained expressive generation.
The four technical chapters instantiate this stance under progressively weaker or different
evidence conditions: symbolic-only completion, score-informed audio refinement, audio-only
score-dynamics recovery, and renderer-conditioned recovery when target-domain velocity labels
are absent.

3.1 Chapter Overview and Position in the Thesis
This chapter addresses Scenario S1, the most controlled setting in the thesis map. Here the
symbolic notes and their timing are already known, but the note-level velocity is missing,
flattened, or musically uninformative. The chapter therefore asks a focused question: can useful
note-level dynamics be recovered from symbolic structure alone, without changing the original
composition?
This setting matters because it isolates the dynamics problem from the broader transcription
problem. It is common in digital audio workstation workflows, step-entered MIDI, quantised
MIDI, and corpus curation settings where note content already exists but expression does not.
It also provides the cleanest test of the thesis claim that dynamics can be treated as a separate
and editable target rather than as a side effect of full expressive performance generation.
This chapter makes three contributions. First, it shows that symbolic velocity completion can
be formulated naturally as an image-colourisation problem on sparse piano-roll representations.
Second, it develops a U-Net-based model with window attention and a sparsity-aware loss
tailored to this representation. Third, it provides both objective and perceptual evidence that
recovering only MIDI velocity can improve musical expressiveness in a controlled setting.
3.2 Task-Specific Related Work
MIDI velocity completion sits at the intersection of expressive rendering and symbolic refinement.
Broad expressive-rendering systems often predict timing, articulation, and dynamics jointly
[79, 80]. These systems are important, but they are not ideal when the practical goal is to
preserve a user’s note content and timing while improving only intensity. A velocity-only
formulation is narrower, but it is also more controllable.
Recent symbolic velocity-prediction methods have typically treated the task as sequential
prediction. Representative examples include convolutional autoencoders and sequence-to-

3.3 Problem Setting
MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) is the dominant symbolic format in modern music
production. A MIDI file that resembles sheet music can still sound mechanical when its
note velocities are undefined or flattened to a default constant. In contrast, MIDI captured
from human performance preserves the performer’s control of intensity and therefore carries a
substantial portion of the perceived musical expression. As discussed in the thesis introduction,
dynamics is the expressive dimension targeted first in this thesis because it is both perceptually
salient and directly editable. This chapter studies the most constrained setting in that map:
recovering note-level MIDI velocity when the symbolic notes and their timing are already known.

This setting is practically important for two reasons. First, a large amount of MIDI used in
digital audio workstation workflows is created by step input, quantisation, or low-cost controllers,
leaving velocity flat. Second, many expressive rendering systems modify several performance
dimensions jointly, including timing and sometimes note content, which is undesirable when
the goal is to preserve the user’s composition. By isolating velocity, the task becomes a precise
and controllable refinement problem: the notes, onsets, and offsets remain fixed, and only note
intensity is completed.
This chapter instead exploits the spatial structure of polyphonic symbolic music by treating
MIDI as an image-colourisation problem. MIDI without velocity is represented as a binary
pianoroll, while the target is a velocity-valued pianoroll. This formulation is especially natural
for instruments such as piano, where multiple simultaneous notes create rich vertical patterns.
Current dataset availability restricts the experiments here to piano data, but the formulation
itself is not tied to piano alone.
3.4 Representation as Image-like MIDI Matrices
3.4.1 Matrix Representation
To process MIDI as images, we convert each performance into three matrices of size T×P, where
T is the number of time frames and P = 88 is the number of piano pitches. The three matrices
are a binary onset roll O, marking note starts; a binary frame roll F, indicating note activation
over time; and a velocity roll V, acting as the target colour intensity. In the implemented
system, F is the network input, V is the prediction target, and O is used for onset masking in
the loss. For the velocity roll, integer values in [0,127] are normalised to [0,1) to match the
model output activation range. During post-processing, the model output is denormalised by
scaling and rounding back to integer MIDI velocity. As illustrated in Figure 3.2, these matrices
are highly sparse.

3.10 Discussion
This chapter has verified that note-level dynamics can be recovered usefully even when the
symbolic notes and timing are held fixed. It turns expressive enhancement into a controllable
post-processing stage rather than a full performance-rendering problem, which is exactly the
setting encountered in many MIDI-production and corpus-curation workflows.
The image-colourisation view is effective because it matches the structure of the data.
Polyphonic note patterns create meaningful vertical and local spatial regularities that are
awkward to express in purely sequential terms. The U-Net captures this local structure well, and
the sparsity-aware loss prevents the model from spending most of its capacity on silent regions.
The results suggest that this representation choice is a major reason the method surpasses the
sequence-based baselines considered here.
The evaluation design is therefore deliberately broader than a single regression score. Ob-
jective error metrics quantify note-wise accuracy, SDvelo checks whether the predicted velocity
distribution retains expressive spread, and the listening test verifies that the recovered layer
changes perceived musical quality. This combination is important because a velocity-completion
model can be numerically close to the target while still sounding too flat, or can sound more
expressive while producing larger point-wise deviations.
The empirical scope of the chapter is piano-based, using datasets recorded with Yamaha
Disklavier instruments. Within that scope, the cross-dataset MAESTRO-to-SMD results show
that the image-colourisation formulation is not merely memorising one corpus.
3.11 Summary
This chapter studied the most controlled form of expressive-label recovery: the notes, onsets, and
offsets are already fixed, and only the missing MIDI velocity layer is inferred. By casting the piano
roll as an image-like representation, the proposed U-Net uses local pitch–time neighbourhoods
and vertical chordal patterns that are weakened in a purely sequential formulation. Window
attention and an onset-masked, velocity-aware loss are not incidental additions; they match the
sparse structure of the target and prevent the model from optimising mainly around silence.
The chapter also shows why velocity completion should not be evaluated only as point-wise
regression. The MAESTRO-to-SMD experiments indicate that the model transfers beyond
its training corpus, while SDvelo and the listening test expose the musical problem that MAE
alone can hide: a prediction may be close to the target and still sound too flat. This makes the
chapter the symbolic-only baseline for the thesis. It preserves the composition exactly, treats
dynamics as an editable layer, and establishes that such a layer has perceptual value before any
audio evidence is introduced.

4.1 Chapter Overview and Position in the Thesis
This chapter addresses Scenario S2 of the thesis. In this setting, a performance audio recording
and an aligned symbolic score are both available. This situation is common in music education,
rehearsal analysis, and curated audio–score archives. The score supplies reliable note content
and approximate temporal structure. However, its expressive information is often incomplete,
simplified, or unreliable. At the same time, modern automatic music transcription (AMT)
systems can estimate note-level MIDI velocity from audio, but these estimates remain noisy and
sensitive to acoustic domain shift. The central question of this chapter is: when note events are
already known from a score, how should score information be used to improve AMT-estimated
velocity without redesigning the whole transcription backbone?
Recent progress in velocity-enabled piano AMT has supported the creation of expressive
piano corpora [6, 117, 132–134] and downstream applications, such as expressive rendering [135],
music education [10], and music generation [136]. Although many audio recordings with aligned
scores are available, reliable note-level dynamics, i.e., MIDI velocity, remain much less available.
In many real collections, a score exists, but its velocity values are either quantised, manually
entered, inherited from notation defaults, or simply unrelated to the performed dynamics. This
creates a natural score-informed correction problem rather than a pure end-to-end transcription
problem.
The present chapter keeps the note-level velocity target but introduces real audio and
aligned score support. AMT backbones evolve rapidly, from CRNN designs to frequency–time
Transformers and event-based models [4, 69, 71, 74]. If each score-informed system requires a
new early-fusion architecture, then any gain in velocity estimation is tightly coupled to one
backbone generation. This chapter instead asks whether score information can be injected late,
as a lightweight correction layer, so that the same idea remains reusable as AMT front ends
change. This design choice is what distinguishes the chapter from earlier score-informed work.

4.2 Task-Specific Related Work and Motivation
Score-informed MIDI velocity estimation developed in parallel with velocity-enabled AMT.
Earlier studies estimated note intensity from piano recordings with hand-crafted acoustic
measurements, statistical analysis, or score-informed factorisation [7, 60, 93, 94]. These studies
established the feasibility of the task, but their dependence on manual parameterisation limited
portability across datasets and recording conditions. More recent work moved to deep learning
and showed that aligned score information can substantially improve note-level dynamics
estimation [23, 61, 83]. The most competitive of these systems use feature fusion, where score
information is injected into the acoustic network during feature extraction.
Those feature-fusion approaches are effective, but they also create a practical bottleneck.
The score-informed mechanism becomes entangled with the acoustic encoder. As a result, the
whole system must usually be re-engineered for each new AMT backbone. This is awkward in
a fast-moving research area where strong transcription front ends already exist and continue
to improve. For a thesis contribution, accuracy alone is not enough. The method should also
expose a reusable principle. This is the main reason for the late-correction stance adopted in
this chapter.
This chapter is also closely related to velocity-enabled piano AMT. Modern systems such as
HPT, HPPNet, hFT-Transformer, and Transkun v2 already estimate MIDI velocity together
with note events [4, 69, 71, 74]. However, audio-only velocity estimation remains acoustically
fragile. Recent analysis shows that transcription models can absorb strong sound and recording
biases, which then harm cross-dataset behaviour [137]. This makes score-informed refinement
attractive even when the AMT backbone is already strong. The role of the aligned score is not
to replace audio. Its role is to provide acoustics-invariant structure that constrains how the
audio-driven estimate should be corrected.
Finally, the chapter should be distinguished from audio–score alignment work. Alignment
studies correct when a note occurs in the score relative to the audio [14, 138, 139]. The present
problem is different. We assume that note content and note timing are already available through
alignment, and we focus on how strongly each aligned note was played. This narrow focus is
deliberate. It lets the chapter isolate note-level dynamics recovery as a standalone problem.
4.3 Problem Setting
The task studied in this chapter is score-informed MIDI velocity estimation. Given a piano
performance audio recording and a time-aligned score, we seek to output MIDI notes with
corrected note-level velocity values. The score is assumed to provide the note events that should
receive a velocity value. The remaining problem is the estimation or correction of velocity at
each note onset.
This setting is practically important for two common use cases. First, educational scenarios
often provide a reference score together with student performance audio. Second, large online
archives often provide aligned audio–score pairs but do not provide trustworthy expressive labels.
52

=== PDF PAGE 80; LABEL 53 ===
In both cases, an audio-only AMT system leaves dynamics analysis incomplete. A score-informed
system can use symbolic structure to stabilise the estimate while retaining acoustic evidence
from the performed sound.
Two assumptions should be stated clearly. First, the method is designed to correct velocity,
not to determine whether a note exists. Second, the score alignment is assumed to be sufficiently
reliable to define note events. This does not mean the score must be perfectly aligned. Indeed,
later sections explicitly test robustness under moderate artificial timing shifts. However, the
problem formulation still presumes that the score and audio refer to the same performed notes.
Relative to the rest of the thesis, this chapter therefore sits between symbolic completion
and higher-level dynamic analysis. Unlike Chapter 3, it operates on real audio and must cope
with acoustically induced error. Unlike Chapter 5, its output remains note-level MIDI velocity
rather than broader dynamic categories or change patterns. This middle position makes it a
useful bridge in the thesis narrative.
4.4 Modular Correction Principle
As discussed in Section 4.2, prior score-informed designs inject score information during acoustic
feature processing and therefore couple the score-informed mechanism to a specific acoustic
encoder. The modular alternative pursued here moves score information to a late-correction
stage that sits after the AMT velocity branch. This relocation gives the design three concrete
properties that are exploited in the rest of the chapter: it preserves compatibility with existing
AMT pipelines, it isolates dynamics refinement from note transcription, and it allows the
same correction module to be reused across acoustically different AMT backbones. Figure 4.1
illustrates the distinction.

The interface of the proposed system is intentionally simple. The AMT backbone first

First, it keeps the acoustic front end reusable. Second, it allows the correction module to be

does not decide whether the note exists.
This separation of roles is important. The acoustic branch still provides the initial evidence
for performance intensity. The score does not overwrite that evidence. Instead, it regularises
the estimate by telling the model where notes occur and how they are organised in time. Later
results will show that this division of labour is strong enough to improve both in-domain accuracy
and cross-dataset behaviour.

4.5.4 Post-Processing
MIDI velocity is defined at note onset. It reflects the instantaneous intensity of the keystroke
rather than a time-varying loudness curve [7]. This onset-centred definition is also consistent
with the physical mechanism of the piano: once a key has been struck, subsequent finger pressure
does not further control the sounding intensity. This differs from bowed strings such as violin,
where bow pressure, bow speed, and vibrato can shape the sound after onset. For piano, the
note-level velocity target is therefore naturally tied to the attack.
In our setting, a time-aligned MIDI score is available, which provides the note positions
including onsets and duration (note frames). We therefore bypass AMT-style note detection and
instead focus only on estimating the velocity value for each given note. During post-processing,
the predicted velocity at the onset is taken and then assigned to the whole note event, consistent
with the MIDI specification.

4.7.2 Cross-dataset generalisation
To study generalisation, we track learning curves on the MAESTRO validation set and on a
held-out 10% subset of SMD, which does not provide an official validation split. Figures 4.6 show
that all score-informed HPT variants outperform the plain HPT baseline on both in-domain
and out-of-domain data. For the Transformer family, the onset + frame condition gives the
strongest overall cross-dataset behaviour, although the difference between score feature variants
remains modest.
A clear pattern appears during training. Performance on MAESTRO improves steadily.
Performance on SMD peaks earlier and then degrades. This indicates that prolonged training
overfits to MAESTRO-specific acoustics. Similar sound biases in AMT velocity estimation were
also reported in [137]. The score-informed module does not remove this problem completely.
However, it shifts the error curves downward and stabilises out-of-domain behaviour. This
supports the view that the aligned score acts as an acoustics-invariant structural prior.
The contrast between the BiLSTM and the Transformer is especially informative. The
BiLSTM tends to fit MAESTRO more aggressively, but its out-of-domain degradation is also
more pronounced. This behaviour is consistent with the way the two modules process the
sequence. The BiLSTM consumes all 1001 frames, including many highly redundant sustain
frames. Adjacent frames often carry very similar acoustic-envelope information. A recurrent
model can therefore reduce training loss by memorising dataset-specific loudness trajectories,
microphone responses, and reverberation patterns that repeat within MAESTRO recordings. Its
bidirectional hidden state also entangles each correction with broad frame-level acoustic context.

event sequence, while the bounded residual update in Eq. (4.5) constrains the magnitude of
each correction. In effect, the Transformer is encouraged to learn where and how much the
preliminary velocity should be adjusted, instead of re-modelling the full acoustic envelope. This
yields a slightly less aggressive in-domain fit than the BiLSTM, but better cross-dataset stability.
For this reason, the Transformer becomes the main correction module in the remainder of the
chapter.

4.7.4 Robustness to audio–score misalignment
Score alignment quality is a central condition for score-informed correction. This chapter
therefore tests that condition directly. To evaluate robustness under imperfect synchronisation,
we conduct timing-shift experiments using artificial score displacements from ±0.1 to ±2.5
seconds, following [23]. These shifts are applied to the SMD evaluation data. For example, a
+0.1 shift means that the original aligned score is intentionally delayed by 0.1 seconds. All
models evaluated here are the same final models used in the cross-backbone comparison above.
They are trained only on perfectly aligned MAESTRO data. The results are summarised in
Figure 4.7.
A methodological point is important here. In the earlier benchmark of [23], shifted scores were
used as model inputs but the original unshifted score was still used during post-processing. That
choice follows the earlier evaluation tradition of [61], but it introduces a temporal inconsistency.
If a model is conditioned on a shifted score, then its output is tied to that shifted note grid.
Post-processing should therefore use the same shifted score. We adopt this internally consistent
protocol in the present chapter. Under this refinement, FiLM U-Net is substantially more robust
than originally implied by the earlier report.
Applying this refined protocol, Score-HPT achieves the strongest robustness curve on SMD.
More broadly, all score-informed methods degrade only moderately relative to the perfectly
aligned case. Given that MIDI velocity spans the range 0 to 127, the observed increase of only
a few units in MAE or SDae under even large global shifts remains small in absolute terms.
Minor shifts can even lead to slight improvements for some proposed variants. A plausible

4.8 Discussion
The main finding of this chapter is that score information is most useful when it is treated as a
structural prior rather than as a full replacement for the acoustic model. The AMT backbone still
supplies the initial estimate of performance intensity. The score-informed module only performs
a targeted correction at onset locations. This division of labour keeps the system lightweight
and explains why the gain transfers across backbones. It also clarifies why score information
helps cross-dataset behaviour. The score does not change with microphone placement, room
acoustics, or recording chain. It regularises a prediction target that is otherwise acoustically
fragile.
The retained BiLSTM experiment makes this point sharper. The BiLSTM results show that
score-informed correction is not inherently tied to attention. A recurrent module can also exploit
score support and can even fit the training domain more strongly. However, the Transformer
is a better final design because its inductive bias matches the task more closely. Velocity is a
note-onset attribute. The onset-token Transformer models note events directly. The BiLSTM
instead processes a long dense frame sequence in which many frames correspond to sustain
and acoustic decay rather than to distinct note decisions. This is precisely where overfitting to
recording-specific envelopes becomes easier. The Transformer therefore trades a small amount

of in-domain fit for better portability and generalisation.
The robustness experiments also refine the role of the alignment assumption. The present test
perturbs the score with a global shift, which is the relevant condition for moderately imperfect
synchronisation in curated score–audio archives. Within this tested condition, late correction
remains stable even when the score grid is displaced by a surprisingly large amount. The
practical bottleneck is therefore narrower than a strict perfect-alignment assumption might
suggest: the method does not require sample-perfect score synchronisation to be useful.
4.9 Summary
This chapter moved from symbolic completion to score-informed audio refinement. The task
is still note-level MIDI velocity, but the estimate now starts from an AMT velocity branch
and is corrected using aligned score structure. The proposed Transformer correction module
deliberately sits late in the pipeline: it does not redesign the acoustic encoder, and it does not
decide which notes exist. It only adjusts the velocity attached to known score onsets.
The main result is therefore a design principle rather than just a lower error table. Aligned
score information acts as an acoustics-invariant prior for a target that is otherwise sensitive to
recording condition and transcription backbone. This explains why the same correction idea
improves HPT, HPPNet, and DynEst, strengthens MAESTRO-to-SMD/MAPS generalisation,
and remains stable under moderate global score–audio shifts. The BiLSTM comparison further
sharpens the point: a denser sequence model can fit the training domain, but the onset-token
Transformer better matches the object being estimated. In the thesis sequence, this chapter
bridges the purely symbolic setting and the later label-scarce setting by showing that score
structure can stabilise velocity recovery without becoming entangled with low-level acoustic
modelling.

5.1 Chapter Overview and Position in the Thesis
Score-level dynamic markings such as p, mf, and f provide the principal human-readable
description of musical intensity in notated music. These markings are inherently relative,
are naturally indexed by the beat grid rather than by individual note onsets, and cannot in
general be inferred from the symbolic score itself. This chapter addresses Scenario S3 of the
thesis, in which performance audio is the only available input and the desired output is a
beat-synchronous symbolic description of dynamics. Recovering such a representation requires
not only the dynamic labels themselves but also the metrical structure needed to anchor them
on a score.
Instead of recovering note-level velocity, which is precise and directly editable, the present
chapter targets a more interpretable score-facing representation. It therefore completes the
thesis map by addressing the setting in which audio is the only available input and the desired
output is a beat-synchronous symbolic description rather than raw note-level control.
5.2 Task-Specific Related Work
Audio-to-score dynamics recovery is related to, but distinct from, note-level velocity estimation.
A large part of the expressive-performance literature studies how performed loudness relates
to symbolic dynamic markings and how those markings vary with musical context [17, 30, 58].
This literature makes two points that matter here. First, score dynamics is musically meaningful
and worth recovering. Second, it is inherently ambiguous and cannot be reduced to a fixed
physical threshold.
A second relevant line of work concerns metrical structure estimation. Beat and downbeat
tracking has a strong literature in MIR, with modern deep models achieving increasingly robust

5.3 Problem Setting and Targets
Chapter 2 already introduced the ambiguity of score dynamics, the distinction between score
dynamics and MIDI velocity, and the role of beats and downbeats as a musically meaningful co-
ordinate. Rather than repeat that background, this chapter focuses on the concrete transcription
problem studied in this work.
Given a piano performance audio signal, the system predicts four synchronised output
sequences on a frame grid: dynamic levels, dynamic change points, beats, and downbeats. The
dynamic targets are the score-facing outputs, while beats and downbeats provide the metrical
structure required to align these labels to a symbolic score. This joint design serves two practical
modes of use. In the first mode, a reliable beat grid is already available from an aligned score or
a score-level AMT system, and the predicted dynamic labels are attached to that grid. In the
second mode, no score is available, and the model first predicts beats and downbeats by itself,
then uses the estimated grid to place the dynamic labels.
More specifically, the dynamic-level target is formulated as a six-class frame-wise prediction
problem, including one blank class before the first annotated marking and the five score-dynamics
classes used in MazurkaBL: pp, p, mf, f, and ff. The change-point target is binary and indicates
whether a contextual transition between adjacent dynamic regimes occurs at a given time. Beats
and downbeats are also binary frame-wise targets. At inference time, the frame-wise outputs
are converted into beat-synchronous score annotations by sampling the dynamic class at beat
locations and snapping detected change points to the nearest beat.
This task directly matches the role assigned to Chapter 5 in the thesis plan: score-level
dynamics should be represented as a beat-synchronous interface comprising dynamic levels,
change points, and metrical structure. It also supports the broader thesis objective of recovering
expressive labels without changing the underlying note content or timing.

5.4 Perceptually Grounded Features and Multi-Scale
Backbone
5.4.1 Audio feature representations
Log-Mel spectrograms serve as the standard input representation in many modern MIR systems,
providing a natural baseline for both dynamics estimation and beat/downbeat tracking tasks. To
ensure a fair comparison with advanced beat tracking models [100, 101], their widely standardised
audio preprocessing configuration is adopted throughout this chapter. Specifically, the audio
is downmixed to mono and resampled to 22.05 kHz. A Hann-windowed STFT is then applied
using a window length of 1024 and a hop size of 441 samples, followed by a projection onto 128
Mel bands. This yields a precise frame rate of 50 fps. Under this setting, the log-Mel input is
represented as XMel ∈R128×T, where T denotes the number of temporal frames after feature
extraction.
Recent studies have started to question whether log-Mel features are the most suitable
representation for musical dynamics. Narang et al. [24] compared log-Mel and Bark-scale
loudness-based features for singing voice dynamics estimation and reported that Bark-scale
features consistently outperformed log-Mel inputs. This observation is particularly relevant to
the present study because score dynamics are closer to perceived intensity than to raw spectral
energy. For piano dynamics, this motivation is also consistent with the use of specific loudness
in earlier studies on performed dynamics [30]. BSSL represents loudness over auditory critical
bands, and therefore keeps the perceptual distribution of intensity across frequency regions. This
makes it more informative than a single integrated loudness curve, while being more compact
and perceptually aligned than a high-dimensional spectral magnitude representation.
A core challenge was the implementation of the BSSL extractor. MOSQITO [142] provides an
ISO 532-1:2017-compliant pipeline [40] appropriate for vocal settings [24], but its 48 kHz-oriented
workflow is overly constrained. This is unsuitable here because the MazurkaBL dataset is natively
44.1 kHz, while the baselines for comparison are all processed under a 22.05 kHz, 50 fps feature
configuration. Using MOSQITO would require an additional resampling (44.1 kHz to 48 kHz)
and different FFT parameters, making the feature comparison unbalanced and less efficient. We
instead implemented a custom BSSL extractor in PyTorch by reproducing the ma sone function
from Pampalk’s Music Analysis MATLAB toolbox [143], following the algorithmic chain in [37].
The mathematical formulation of this extractor is given in Chapter 2. Using the same STFT
parameters as the log-Mel input, the BSSL extractor produces XBSSL ∈R22×T. We use the full
BSSL matrix rather than compressing it into the BSTL, because the latter would discard the
frequency-band structure that may carry useful timbral and register-dependent cues for piano
dynamics.

5.4.2 Shared multi-scale encoder
A central modelling challenge is the conflict between the temporal context and frame-level
precision required by the four tasks. While metrical tasks (beats and downbeats) rely on
high temporal resolution to localise sharp transients [99], dynamics-related tasks (dynamics
and change points) depend on the surrounding phrase, local contrast, register, and relative
loudness context [24], thereby requiring extended context lengths and stable spectral features. We
therefore adapt the multi-scale network of [102] as the shared encoder. Since this architecture was
originally designed for playing techniques spanning different temporal scales, it is exceptionally
well-equipped to resolve the competing receptive-field requirements of our four tasks.
As shown in Fig. 5.2, given an F ×T time–frequency feature, where F is the number
of frequency bands and T is the number of time frames, the encoder first applies 2D batch
normalisation and then forms three temporal branches at resolutions T, T/s, and T/s2, where
s denotes the temporal scaling factor. The full-resolution branch preserves frame-level timing
evidence, the middle branch provides an intermediate representation, and the coarsest branch
gives the largest effective receptive field. Following [102], residual blocks are used for local
feature extraction, while self-attention blocks are applied to strengthen long-range temporal
modelling. After multi-scale fusion, the encoder outputs a shared latent sequence Z ∈RT×8
,
which is then passed to the MMoE decoder and task-specific heads.
Unlike the reference architecture which fixes the scaling factor to 3 [102], we treat s as
a tunable hyperparameter to explicitly control the trade-off between temporal precision and
contextual abstraction. If s is too small, the three branches become insufficiently differentiated
and the encoder behaves close to a single-resolution model with redundant side branches. If s is
too large, the coarsest branch provides stronger context but may over-compress information that
is still useful for event localisation. Under our 50 fps feature rate, the empirically best value
s = 5 means that the coarsest branch operates at approximately 2 fps, giving the attention
blocks a substantially longer temporal view while the full-rate branch still preserves 20 ms
frame-level timing. The ablation study in Section 5.7.2 discusses this effect in more detail.
The feature choice also interacts with the model design. Using 22-band BSSL instead of
128-bin log-Mel reduces the frequency dimension entering the convolutional encoder, lowering
memory usage and making longer segments feasible. This allows the model to capture phrase-level
dynamics while remaining compact, as further confirmed in Section 5.7.2 Ablation Study.

5.6 Experimental Setup
5.6.1 Dataset and protocol
We use the MazurkaBL dataset [96], a score-aligned corpus of 2,098 solo piano recordings covering
44 Chopin Mazurkas. After excluding two mazurkas with irregular dynamics annotations (M06-4
and M63-2), 1,999 recordings are used. Although newer datasets such as EME33 [144] exist,
MazurkaBL remains the largest publicly available resource for studying notated dynamics
against performed loudness from audio. Its provision of score-aligned beat times and verified
expressive markings makes it particularly suitable for the task of beat-synchronous score-
dynamics transcription. The choice to train on MazurkaBL is therefore deliberate: among
available solo-piano corpora, it provides the most complete supervision for the joint target
studied in this chapter, rather than only note-level velocity or metrical annotations.
For cross-dataset evaluation, we additionally use the Vienna4x22 Piano Corpus [120] and
Batik-plays-Mozart [121]. Both external corpora are used only as test sets. Vienna4x22 contains
repeated performances of four classical piano excerpts, whereas Batik-plays-Mozart contains
movement-level Mozart piano performances with matched symbolic information. They are
converted into the same HDF5-style schema as MazurkaBL, including waveform, beat, downbeat,
measure, change-point, and dynamic-label fields. The conversion uses the available MusicXML
scores and score–performance match files, so that all three corpora can be evaluated with the
same post-processing and metric code. This design keeps the cross-dataset experiment focused
on corpus transfer rather than on differences in file format or evaluation implementation.
Table 5.1 summarises the five-level dynamic-label distributions used by the evaluation
pipeline. It is included because the three corpora share the same score-dynamics vocabulary
but differ markedly in their label priors. MazurkaBL has a large p class and a substantial loud
region; Vienna4x22 is strongly concentrated in pp, p, and mf ; Batik-plays-Mozart is concentrated
mainly around p and f. A classifier trained only on MazurkaBL therefore learns not only an
acoustics-to-symbol mapping, but also the prior probability of each symbolic label under the
Chopin-mazurka annotation convention.
We use 5-fold cross-validation stratified at the mazurka-opus level. This split is deliberately
piece-aware: performances of the same mazurka do not appear in both training and test folds.
Given the limited size of the corpus, this provides a data-efficient way to test generalisation to
unseen pieces within MazurkaBL, while avoiding the overly optimistic estimates that can arise
when different performances of the same work leak across splits. For each fold, the model is
trained for 120 epochs, and the best checkpoint is selected on the validation set using F1 score.
Optimisation uses AdamW with learning rate 3 ×10−4, batch size 10, and fixed random seed 86.

5.7.1 Main results
Table 5.2 shows that the proposed multi-task framework gives the strongest overall results on

5.7.4 Discussion: why score dynamics is not a relabelled MIDI
velocity problem
The objective in this chapter is not note-level control of a synthesiser but a score-facing summary
of expressive intensity on a musically meaningful coordinate. Score dynamics is therefore not
obtained here by simply discretising an underlying velocity curve. It is learned as a symbolic
description of dynamic regimes together with the metrical structure needed to place that
description on a score.
This distinction explains several design choices. The model uses longer context because
phrase-level dynamic intent extends beyond local note attacks. The post-processing snaps
change points to beats because the available annotations are editorial and metrical rather than
freely continuous. The evaluation is beat-synchronous because a musically meaningful score
label should be judged on the score grid, not only on raw frame precision.
The cross-dataset results make this distinction more concrete. If score dynamics were merely
a continuous loudness variable relabelled into five fixed bins, an out-of-domain corpus with
compatible audio quality would mainly cause smooth calibration errors. Instead, Vienna4x22

Note-level velocity and score dynamics serve different purposes. Note-level velocity is the

dynamics is the more interpretable target when the goal is symbolic annotation, educational
feedback, or performance analysis.
5.7.5 Limitations
Several limitations should be kept in mind. First, the dynamic vocabulary is restricted to
the five static score levels present in MazurkaBL plus a blank class. The model does not
explicitly represent longer-span symbols such as crescendo and diminuendo; instead, it captures
local transitions through change points. Second, the beat-aligned post-processing reflects the
annotation style of MazurkaBL. This is musically sensible for this dataset, but it may not
transfer directly to corpora with different editorial conventions.
Third, although the chapter now includes Vienna4x22 and Batik-plays-Mozart, the empirical
scope is still solo piano. The two external corpora test distribution shift within classical piano,
not immediate generalisation to other instruments or mixed ensembles. They are also much
smaller than MazurkaBL, so their results should be interpreted as transfer diagnostics rather
than as a complete benchmark of score-dynamics transcription.
A related future direction is to connect the present multi-task framework with the broader
training regime used in modern beat and downbeat tracking. Systems such as Beat This [101]
are designed for general beat/downbeat estimation rather than for score-dynamics transcription,
and the broader beat-tracking corpora available for such systems may strengthen the metrical
heads of a transformer-based or multi-scale model. However, those corpora generally do not
provide compatible beat-synchronous score-dynamics labels or change-point annotations. Adding
them would therefore change the problem into a partially supervised multi-task setting, where
some datasets supervise only beats/downbeats while MazurkaBL-like corpora supervise the full
dynamics structure. This is a promising extension, but it is separate from the fully supervised
score-dynamics experiment completed in this chapter.
Finally, the feature comparison suggests that no single front-end is uniformly optimal across
all tasks or corpora. BSSL is the better global choice for the present chapter because it gives
the strongest in-domain balance and a much smaller model, but downbeat tracking and some
external transfer cases still benefit from log-Mel input. Looking forward, the Rach3 dataset
[145] provides rehearsal-style annotations across a broader repertoire and is currently still under
construction. Once at usable scale, Rach3 would offer a complementary validation target and
an additional source for continued training or calibration of the framework presented here.

5.8 Summary
This chapter changed the target representation from note-level MIDI velocity to score-level
dynamics. The aim is no longer to control a synthesiser note by note, but to recover a beat-
synchronous symbolic description that can be written back to a score. This distinction motivates
the whole design: BSSL is used because dynamics are closer to perceived loudness than to raw
spectral magnitude; the multi-scale encoder provides both phrase-level context and frame-level
timing; and the four heads jointly estimate dynamic levels, change points, beats, and downbeats
because the dynamic label is only meaningful once it is placed on a metrical coordinate.
The results should be read in that representational frame. On MazurkaBL, the compact
BSSL-based multi-task model provides a strong in-domain benchmark with far fewer parameters
than the log-Mel variant. The external Vienna4x22 and Batik-plays-Mozart tests then show the
harder limitation: score-dynamics transfer is not only acoustic transfer. It also depends on label
priors and editorial conventions, so dynamic-label transfer and metrical-structure transfer can
fail in different ways. This chapter therefore answers the thesis requirement for a score-facing
coordinate, while also clarifying why score dynamics is not simply MIDI velocity binned into
five loudness classes.

6.1 Chapter Overview and Position in the Thesis
This chapter extends the thesis’s velocity-recovery work beyond piano. The two earlier velocity
chapters established complementary methods within the supervised piano regime: Chapter 3
addressed Scenario S1 by completing missing MIDI velocities from symbolic structure alone, and
Chapter 4 addressed Scenario S2 by refining audio-derived velocities using an aligned score. Both
relied on the same supervisory anchor: MAESTRO [5], in which note-aligned velocity labels
are available because the performances were captured on sensor-equipped Yamaha Disklavier
instruments. Outside piano, this anchor disappears. Public datasets for guitar, violin, and other
acoustic instruments [20, 123, 124] provide audio and aligned notes but no note-level velocity
labels. Supervised training and label-based domain transfer are therefore both unavailable in
the target domain.
The central question of this chapter is therefore: how can note-level MIDI velocities be
recovered from real performance audio for instruments where ground-truth velocity labels are not
accessible? The answer pursued here is analysis-by-synthesis: predict velocities whose rendered
dynamics are consistent with the observed performance audio, and drive the prediction with
gradients passed through a differentiable interface to a renderer.
This framing leads to two candidate supervision interfaces. The first is a differentiable
waveform synthesiser (Diff-Synth) that renders predicted velocities into audio and matches
them against the real recording in waveform or spectral space. The second is a differentiable
SoundFont proxy (Diff-SFProxy) that maps predicted velocities to note-wise loudness-related
acoustic parameters and matches those parameters against measurements of the real audio.
Both interfaces share the same front-end estimator and the same regularisers, so the comparison
isolates the effect of the supervision signal itself.

6.2 Task-Specific Related Work and Motivation
MIDI velocity is the per-note control value that most symbolic music systems use to represent
how strongly a note is played. In synthesisers, velocity explicitly controls behaviour such as
amplitude, sample selection, and filtering. In acoustic instruments, velocity has a clear physical
effect on perceptual loudness but is rarely captured directly, except on sensor-equipped pianos.
Reliable velocity labels are crucial for expressive rendering [135, 146], music generation [136, 147],
performance analysis [148], and data curation [117].
Most publicly available MIDI files lack velocity driven by human performance. A common
practice in music software is a rule-based mapping from score-level dynamic markings such
as p and f (where p stands for piano, soft, and f stands for forte, loud) to fixed velocity
values, or defaulting to 64 if no marking is present [7]. Such velocities carry minimal expressive
information.
Velocity estimation has so far been almost exclusively a piano problem. Learning-
based velocity estimation from performance audio recovers expressive labels well, but almost
entirely in the piano domain [23, 94, 95]. The reason is data: piano datasets such as MAE-
STRO [5] provide note-aligned velocity labels sourced from Yamaha Disklavier performances.
For guitar, violin, and many other instruments, public datasets [20, 123, 124] provide audio and
aligned notes but no velocity labels. This precludes both supervised training and label-based
domain transfer. The asymmetry between piano and other instruments is therefore not primarily
a modelling gap but a labelling gap, and any method that aims to address the gap must operate
without ground-truth note-level velocity supervision in the target domain.
Synthetic-data transfer for transcription. A common response to label scarcity in tran-
scription is to train on synthesised audio, where symbolic labels are available by construction,
86

=== PDF PAGE 114; LABEL 87 ===
and then transfer the model to real performances [86, 116, 149]. Some recent works follow this
route specifically to obtain synthetic velocity supervision [150, 151].
This chapter takes a different and simpler route. Instead of training on synthesised audio
with synthetic labels, the front-end estimator is trained directly against real performance audio.
The synthesiser is involved at training time only as a differentiable interface that connects
predicted velocities to a measurable property of the real recording. This avoids the synthesis-to-
performance domain gap that dominates synthetic-data transfer pipelines, at the cost of needing
a differentiable supervision interface that is reliable across the instruments of interest.
Differentiable waveform synthesisers. Differentiable digital signal processing provides
a flexible route for embedding synthesis inside end-to-end learning [106]. The same idea has
been extended to instrument-specific waveform synthesisers, including DDSP-Piano [109] and
DDSP-Guitar-Synth [111], both of which accept MIDI-oriented inputs that include per-note
velocity. Such models can render predicted velocities into audio and propagate gradients from a
waveform-domain loss back into a velocity estimator. The DDSP family also includes integrated
systems with detailed expressive control [108].
Two limitations of waveform-level supervision are relevant here. First, a Diff-Synth only
approximates the real instrument; its waveform statistics differ from real recordings in timbre,
articulation, microphone response, and room acoustics. A waveform or spectral loss therefore
spends much of its gradient budget on residual mismatch rather than on velocity-dependent
behaviour. Second, an instrument-specific Diff-Synth requires a dedicated design: piano-
oriented synthesisers expect piano-specific controls such as pedal state, while guitar-oriented
synthesisers expect string-wise trajectories. Reusing one Diff-Synth across instruments is
therefore impractical.
Neural proxies for black-box audio systems. Because standard SoundFont rendering is
non-differentiable, gradients cannot pass through it directly, which motivates a neural proxy.
Mart´ınez Ram´ırez et al. [112] demonstrated differentiable signal processing with black-box
audio effects. Barkan et al. [152] introduced InverSynth II, which learns a differentiable
synthesiser proxy for sound matching and combines it with self-supervised inference-time
finetuning. Combes et al. [153] studied neural proxies that map presets to perceptually informed
audio embeddings for black-box software synthesisers. These works show that surrogate networks
can bridge a non-differentiable renderer and gradient-based optimisation.
The proxy used in this chapter follows the same general strategy but is adapted to the
velocity-estimation task. Unlike the audio-embedding proxies of prior work, the proxy here
does not aim to reproduce waveforms or perceptual embeddings of complete sounds. Instead,
it predicts a small set of note-wise loudness-related acoustic parameters that are extracted by
the same procedure from both proxy outputs and real recordings. The two design benefits are
clear. First, many SoundFonts can plug in off the shelf, whereas Diff-Synth requires a dedicated
design per instrument. Second, gradients focus on velocity-relevant intensity and attack cues
rather than on waveform details that are dominated by recording and timbral mismatch.

note velocity is directly observed at performance time. For many non-piano acoustic recordings,
however, no canonical ground-truth MIDI velocity is attached to the performance. The reason
is physical. A guitarist’s pluck or a violinist’s bowing involves richer continuous control than
a single onset scalar can describe, including pluck force and angle, bow speed and pressure,
contact point, and articulation. A one-dimensional MIDI velocity is therefore not a universal
physical truth for these instruments.

framing. For a target instrument, let Rdenote a chosen renderer that exposes a per-note velocity

6.4 Cross-Instrument Velocity Estimation Framework
Figure 6.1 summarises the framework. VeloEst is first trained in the piano domain using ground-
truth velocity labels. The piano-pretrained checkpoint is then adapted to the target instrument
through one of two differentiable interfaces. The Diff-Synth interface renders predicted velocities
into waveforms and matches them against the real audio with a multi-scale spectral loss. The Diff-
SFProxy interface maps predicted velocities to note-wise loudness-related acoustic parameters
and matches them against parameters extracted from the real audio. During adaptation, only
VeloEst parameters are updated; both back-ends remain frozen.
The two interfaces share a common training objective. They differ only in the main loss
term, in the back-end being frozen, and in the output space they compare against. This shared
structure is deliberate. It allows the comparison between waveform-level and parameter-level
supervision to be attributed to the supervision interface rather than to incidental differences in
optimisation or regularisation.

6.5.2 Proxy Architecture
Figure 6.2 shows the proxy architecture. A Transformer encoder maps a padded sequence of up
to Nmax = 64 note events in a 5 s segment to the corresponding (PHEn,OSFn) pairs. The choice
Nmax = 64 is motivated by a data analysis on MAESTRO showing that human performances
rarely exceed 64 notes per 5 s.
Each note token combines three components. The first is a learnable pitch embedding. The
second is a shared learnable basis that maps onset, duration, and velocity (each normalised to
[0,1]) to additive directions in token space. The third is a two-layer MLP with GELU activation
on the normalised onset, acting as a learnable positional encoding. These components are
summed and passed through LayerNorm to form the note token. Padded slots are then zeroed.
The encoder stacks six pre-norm Transformer layers (d= 256, 8 heads, FFN width 4d= 1024,
GELU, dropout 0.1), with a key-padding mask excluding padded slots from attention. A shared
note-wise head (d →d →2, with GELU) emits each (PHEn,OSFn) pair. The encoder-only
design fits this set-to-set regression task naturally, because the proxy has no autoregressive
generation requirement: each note’s targets depend on the full event context but are produced
in parallel. All three segment-length variants of the proxy (2/5/10 s, with Nmax set to 32/64/128
accordingly) share the same architecture and yield approximately 4.9 M parameters.

6.5.3 MIDI Segment Sampling
The proxy is trained entirely on procedurally generated MIDI segments. Each segment is a list
of note events {(pn,tn,ℓn,vn)}N
n=1 in which pitch, onset, duration, and velocity vn ∈[0,1] are all
written by the sampler rather than measured from any recording. The velocities therefore serve
both as the proxy’s input control variable during training and as the recoverable target in the
velocity-recovery diagnostic of Section 6.7; this is the only sense in which a per-note velocity
“ground truth” exists in this chapter.
Note events come from a three-component mixture, weighted 0.5/0.3/0.2.
Coverage component (50%). Ordinary polyphonic textures: pitches uniform over the
MIDI piano range [21,108], polyphony capped at 14, durations uniform in [0.08,0.80] s, and
inter-onset intervals uniform in [0.03,0.35] s. Per-note velocities are stratified between uniform,
near-boundary, and extreme values.
Boundary component (30%). A low-polyphony pass (cap of 2, chord probability 0.12) with
velocities biased towards the SoundFont’s velocity-layer boundaries. SoundFonts internally store
a small number of discrete sample layers, one per velocity range, and a small velocity change
near a layer boundary produces a sharp acoustic transition rather than a smooth gain change.
The proxy must learn these transitions, and this component concentrates training mass on them.
Stress component (20%). Deliberately adversarial: polyphony up to 16, chord size up to 8,
inter-onset intervals as short as 0.01 s, and velocities biased towards the extremes of [0,1].
Within any chord, in all three components, per-note velocities are sampled independently,
so the proxy is trained to produce per-note rather than chord-uniform velocity responses.

6.6 Experimental Setup
The experiments are organised around two questions. First, can the frozen Diff-SFProxy provide
useful gradients for velocity recovery when the true velocity is known in synthetic data? This is a
precondition for the next experiment and is reported in Section 6.7. Second, when the front-end
VeloEst model is adapted using real target-domain audio, does Diff-SFProxy provide a better
supervision interface than waveform-domain Diff-Synth? This is the main empirical question of
the chapter, reported in Section 6.8. The two experiments use different evaluation metrics: the
diagnostic uses synthetic per-note velocity MAE, defined inline in Section 6.7.2, whereas the
adaptation experiment uses perceptual loudness metrics, defined in Section 6.8.2. The present
section defines only the datasets and the SoundFont assets shared by both experiments.
6.6.1 Datasets
The experiments use piano as the source domain, where velocity labels are available, and guitar
as the main label-scarce target domain.
For piano, training and in-domain evaluation use the MAESTRO V3 dataset [5], which
contains 198.7 h of Yamaha Disklavier performances with note-aligned MIDI velocities. The
default train/valid/test split is followed throughout. Cross-dataset evaluation uses the SMD
dataset [118], which contains 4.7 h of additional Yamaha Disklavier performances with the same
label format.
For guitar, adaptation uses the GAPS dataset [124], which contains 14 h of nylon-string
classical guitar with aligned notes but no velocity labels, again using its default train/valid/test
split. Cross-dataset evaluation uses the Fran¸cois Leduc (FL) dataset [123], which contains 4.0 h
of solo jazz guitar recordings on a mix of nylon-string, steel-string, electric, and acoustic guitars.
The two guitar corpora differ substantially in playing style, recording chain, and instrument
variety, which makes FL a useful test of out-of-distribution behaviour for guitar adaptation.
This dataset configuration has a deliberate asymmetry. On piano, ground-truth velocities
are available on both the adaptation and the cross-dataset evaluation set, which permits direct
error metrics. On guitar, no ground-truth velocities are available on either set, which forces a
different evaluation protocol based on perceptual loudness comparison (Section 6.8.2).

6.7 Diff-SFProxy Velocity-Recovery Diagnostic
Before deploying Diff-SFProxy in cross-instrument adaptation, we test whether its gradients
can be trusted. The question is direct: do the proxy’s gradients point velocity in the right
direction? The answer is obtained through a controlled inversion on synthetic audio, where
the true velocities are known by construction. The test segments are drawn from the same
procedural sampler as the proxy’s training data (Section 6.5.3), so the velocities are written
into each segment by the sampler rather than measured from any recording. This holds for
any instrument-SoundFont pair, including guitar, which is why a per-note velocity MAE is
reported for both piano and guitar proxies in Table 6.1. The setting also isolates the gradient
pathway from any real-audio domain gap. A pass on this diagnostic is a precondition for the
cross-instrument adaptation experiments in Section 6.8.
6.7.1 Training Protocol
Each proxy is trained on 20k MIDI segments, with an additional 2k for validation. The optimiser
is AdamW with a learning rate of 10−4, weight decay 0.01, batch size 64, and a fixed random
seed of 19. Diff-SFProxy is trained for 200 epochs; the validation loss typically plateaus between
epochs 150 and 200. The checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is selected for each run. To
accommodate the three input segment lengths 2/5/10 s, Nmax is set to 32/64/128, respectively.
All three proxies use the same Transformer architecture, resulting in approximately 4.9 M
parameters each.

Synthetic MIDI segments are rendered with the chosen SoundFont, and their PHE/OSF
parameters are extracted as the target. All note velocities are then reset to 64, the proxy
is frozen, and gradient descent is run on the candidate velocities until the proxy’s predicted

6.8.1 Training Protocol
Three settings are compared on the same VeloEst front-end: the piano-pretrained baseline
applied without further training, Diff-Synth adaptation, and Diff-SFProxy adaptation. The
Diff-Synth back-ends [109, 111] were originally implemented for 3 s input segments. Each is
retrained at 2 s and 5 s with its original hyperparameters, then frozen, so that crop length is
comparable across the two adaptation interfaces.
During adaptation, VeloEst processes 10 s segments and outputs a frame-wise velocity roll.
The frozen back-end processes shorter crops, using 5 s by default and 2 s for ablation. Back-end
losses are averaged over valid notes in each crop and back-propagated through the onset-frame
velocity reads to the full velocity roll, which keeps the gradient scale comparable across crop
lengths. Adaptation is run separately on the MAESTRO and GAPS training sets for 120k
iterations using Adam with learning rate 10−4, batch size 8, and step decay 0.9 every 10k
iterations. SMD and FL datasets are used only for evaluation. All runs use seed 19, and the
final checkpoint is selected by validation rBSSL on MAESTRO for piano and on GAPS for guitar.
6.8.2 Evaluation Metrics
For piano datasets with ground-truth velocities, we follow [23] and report per-note MAEVelo
on the MIDI scale [0,127], computed against the true note velocities. For datasets without
velocity labels, MAEVelo cannot be computed. We instead re-render the aligned notes through
the selected SoundFont using the predicted velocities and compare the resynthesis with the
original recording in perceptual loudness space.
The comparison features are Bark-scale specific loudness (BSSL) and Bark-scale total loudness
(BSTL), as defined within the Zwicker psychoacoustic framework [29]. BSSL is a frame-wise
critical-band representation, and BSTL is its band-summed contour, tracking perceived loudness
over time. Both features capture the main perceptual effect targeted by velocity estimation,
98

=== PDF PAGE 126; LABEL 99 ===
namely the temporal shaping of loudness, while reducing sensitivity to timbral and recording-
condition mismatch between real audio and SoundFont resynthesis. Two natural alternatives
are deliberately avoided. Raw RMS energy ignores frequency-dependent perceptual sensitivity.
Note-wise loudness measures, including the PHE and OSF used inside the proxy, are restricted
to onset-locked windows and miss the continuous dynamics shape carried by velocity expression.
They also coincide with the proxy training signal, which would make the evaluation circular.
Pearson correlation r between real and rendered loudness contours is the primary metric. It
is invariant to global scale and offset, so it discounts loudness differences introduced by gain,
microphone response, or renderer calibration. Pearson correlation computed on BSSL and BSTL
is denoted rBSSL and rBSTL, respectively. Cosine similarity (cs) and MAE on these features are
retained as diagnostic alternatives: cosine similarity is scale-invariant but not offset-invariant,
while MAE is sensitive to both. Their behaviour is examined in Section 6.9.

6.9 Discussion
6.9.1 What the Recovered Velocity Actually Represents
Velocity is not the only MIDI control that shapes rendered audio loudness. Pedal, reverb, and
continuous controllers all contribute in parallel [18]. Diff-Synth back-ends such as DDSP-Piano
expose several of these controls. Consequently, when adaptation updates only velocity, the
velocity gradient can become entangled with residuals caused by fixed or unobserved controls.
Diff-SFProxy avoids this contamination by treating velocity as the only loudness-shaping degree
of freedom, but the choice carries its own cost. The recovered velocity absorbs effects that, in
the real performance, came from other parallel controls.
The recovered velocity is therefore not a canonical property of the acoustic performance. It
is defined relative to the chosen SoundFont as the velocity that best reproduces the perceptual

6.9.3 When Diff-Synth Should Still Be Preferred
Diff-SFProxy is not a universal replacement for Diff-Synth. Three conditions favour the waveform
interface. First, the matched differentiable synthesiser must exist for the target instrument and
expose a faithful per-note velocity control. Second, its waveform statistics must be acoustically
close to the target recordings. Third, the supervision must be allowed to reach beyond velocity
and update auxiliary controls such as pedal or articulation. When all three conditions hold,
waveform supervision can in principle deliver richer gradients than parameter supervision, and
the present chapter does not contest this in general terms.
The claim of this chapter is narrower. In many cross-instrument settings, none of these three
conditions holds robustly. A matched Diff-Synth may not exist for the target instrument, may be
expensive to retrain at the desired crop length, or may differ from the real recordings in timbre
and recording chain. A practical SoundFont, by contrast, is widely available, plug-and-play, and
instrument-agnostic at the proxy-design level. In that regime, a note-wise proxy that keeps only
the subset of synthesised behaviour most related to velocity can be the more useful engineering
choice. This is the regime in which the present chapter is positioned.
6.9.4 Limitations
Several boundaries should be kept in mind. First, the recovered velocity is renderer-conditioned
(Section 6.9.1). The SoundFont is selected by ear from a small candidate pool. A mismatched
SoundFont would produce a teacher signal that the target recordings cannot match, so renderer
choice is part of the operational definition rather than an incidental detail.
Second, the experiments focus on piano and guitar. Both instruments are predominantly
percussive in their attack profile, which favours the OSF parameter. Sustained-bow instruments
such as violin, or breath-driven instruments such as flute, would require acoustic parameters
that capture sustain and continuous-modulation cues beyond the PHE/OSF pair used here.
The claim is therefore tied to the tested attack-driven setting.
Third, the framework treats velocity as a single one-dimensional control. As noted in
Section 6.3, this is an inherent simplification for many acoustic instruments, where multiple
continuous controls shape note dynamics. The recovered velocity is therefore best read as a
coarse note-level dynamics coordinate rather than as a complete description of expressive intent.
Fourth, the framework relies on aligned note events. Onset and duration errors propagate
into the PHE/OSF windows and into the Diff-Synth rendering, and would degrade adaptation
accordingly. The chapter assumes a score-informed setting in which alignment is already good
enough for note-wise analysis, keeping the experiment focused on the supervision interface rather

6.10 Summary
This chapter returned to note-level MIDI velocity, but removed the condition that made
supervised piano work possible: ground-truth velocity labels in the target domain. The proposed
framework treats beyond-piano velocity estimation as a missing-supervision problem. A piano-
pretrained VeloEst supplies the prior, while adaptation is driven through a renderer interface.
The comparison between Diff-Synth and Diff-SFProxy shows that the useful question is not
simply whether the renderer is differentiable, but whether its gradient is concentrated on the
control variable being estimated.
Diff-SFProxy is preferred in the tested guitar setting because it supervises velocity through
note-wise loudness-related parameters rather than through waveform reconstruction. This
reduces the influence of timbre, room, microphone, and renderer mismatch, and makes the
recovered velocity a renderer-conditioned dynamics coordinate rather than a claim about the
player’s physical gesture. The ablations are important here: without piano pretraining and
anti-collapse regularisation, the model can drift toward median or near-constant solutions.
The chapter therefore closes the velocity branch of the thesis under the weakest supervision
assumption considered here: aligned note events remain available, but the velocity target itself
must be constructed through a perceptually grounded proxy.

Chapter 7
Conclusion
This thesis studied dynamics recovery as a problem of missing expressive labels under structural
preservation. The starting point was not the general observation that musical performance is
expressive, but the narrower problem that many useful music representations already contain
reliable structure while lacking a dynamics layer. A MIDI file may contain the correct notes but
flat velocities. An AMT output may recover note events but not the intensity attached to them.
A score-level transcription may contain metre and pitch content but no dynamic markings. A
beyond-piano dataset may provide audio and aligned notes but no trustworthy velocity labels.
The thesis question introduced in Chapter 1 was:
How can musically interpretable dynamics labels be recovered from
incomplete, weakly expressive, or weakly supervised music data without
rewriting note content or note timing?
The answer developed in this thesis is conditional rather than universal. Dynamics is not a
single target shared across all settings. It may be a note-level MIDI control, a beat-synchronous
score marking, a perceived-loudness contour used for evaluation, or a renderer-conditioned
coordinate constructed under missing supervision. A valid recovery method must therefore
preserve the trusted musical structure, choose the output coordinate appropriate to the target
representation, and evaluate the result in the space where that representation has musical
meaning.
This thesis builds that answer through four controlled recovery settings. Chapter 3 studies
symbolic-only MIDI velocity completion. Chapter 4 studies score-informed correction of audio-
derived MIDI velocity. Chapter 5 studies audio-only recovery of beat-synchronous score dynamics
and metrical structure. Chapter 6 extends velocity recovery beyond the supervised piano regime
through renderer-conditioned proxy supervision. These chapters form a scenario map rather
than one end-to-end pipeline: each preserves a different structural coordinate and asks what
kind of dynamics label can be recovered under that condition.

7. CONCLUSION
7.1 Summary of Contributions
The thesis makes one formulation-level contribution and four method-level contributions. To-
gether, they are best read as instantiations of the same principle: the recoverable dynamics
layer depends on what structure is trusted and what supervision is available. The thesis does
not propose a single model for all forms of expression. It defines controlled recovery settings
and studies the representation, model design, and evaluation method appropriate to each.
7.1.1 A unified formulation of dynamics-oriented label recovery
The first contribution is the thesis-level formulation itself. Chapter 2 established that the gap
between loudness, MIDI velocity, and score dynamics is not a minor conversion problem. A fixed
mapping from velocity to p, mf, or f is attractive because it is simple, but it collapses several
distinct stages: performer action, instrument response, acoustic signal, auditory perception, and
score-level interpretation. The thesis therefore treats each dynamics representation as a valid
but partial coordinate on expression.
This formulation leads to a clear division of tasks. If the target is note-level MIDI velocity,
the system should work on note onsets and preserve note identity. If the target is score dynamics,
the system should work on a musical coordinate such as beats or phrases, not arbitrary audio
frames alone. If the supervision is indirect, the recovered label must be interpreted relative to
the proxy that supplies the learning signal. These distinctions are simple, but they prevent a
large conceptual error: treating dynamics as a scalar loudness value that can be moved freely
between audio, MIDI, and notation.

7.1.2 Symbolic MIDI velocity completion
The first method contribution establishes the most controlled setting: the symbolic note sequence
is fixed, and only the missing MIDI velocity layer is restored. Chapter 3 reformulates this
task as image colourisation on sparse piano-roll matrices. This is not only an architectural
analogy. Polyphonic symbolic music contains local pitch–time neighbourhoods and vertical
chordal patterns that are weakened when the task is treated as a plain sequence. A U-Net
with window attention and a sparsity-aware loss can exploit this structure while leaving the
underlying note content unchanged.
The chapter also demonstrates that a decoupled expressive layer has perceptual value. The
model changes only velocity while preserving notes and timing, yet the listening test shows a
clear improvement over flat and sequence-oriented baselines. This supports the central thesis
stance: expressive recovery does not always require full expressive generation. In many practical
settings, a controlled post-processing layer is the more appropriate object.
7.1.3 Score-informed velocity correction
The second method contribution introduces performance audio while keeping the note structure
fixed through an aligned score. Chapter 4 proposes a modular correction strategy: keep the
AMT backbone unchanged, then use score information as a late residual correction signal
for note-level velocity. This design is deliberately different from early-fusion score-informed
transcription. It treats score information as a structural prior rather than as a replacement for
acoustic evidence.
The empirical result is that the late score-informed module adds only a small parameter
overhead on top of an existing AMT backbone, transfers across HPT, HPPNet, and DynEst
with consistent improvements in MAESTRO-to-SMD cross-dataset behaviour, and remains
stable under moderate audio–score timing shifts. The deeper thesis result is about modularity.
If expressive AMT is to remain useful as AMT front ends evolve, dynamics recovery should
not depend on a single fixed transcription architecture. A late correction layer preserves this
modularity while still using the musical information contained in the score.
7.1.4 Audio-only score-dynamics recovery
The third method contribution changes the target itself from note-level MIDI velocity to score-
facing dynamic markings. Chapter 5 therefore requires a different temporal coordinate. Score
dynamics is not naturally a note-onset variable; it describes dynamic regimes, changes, and
phrase-level intensity states. The chapter formulates the task as joint estimation of dynamic
levels, dynamic change points, beats, and downbeats because the dynamic label is only useful
once it is placed on a writable metrical grid.
This chapter provides the score-dynamics counterpart to the velocity-focused chapters. It
uses Bark-scale specific loudness as a perceptually grounded input representation, paired with a
multi-scale encoder and an MMoE decoder that resolve the conflict between long-range dynamic

estimated directly from audio when the model also estimates the coordinate on which those
labels are meaningful. The point is not that score dynamics is a discretised version of MIDI

7.1.5 Beyond-piano velocity recovery through proxy supervision
The fourth method contribution removes the strongest assumption used by supervised piano
work: target-domain velocity labels. Chapter 6 treats beyond-piano velocity estimation as a
missing-supervision problem rather than ordinary domain shift. Piano has an unusually strong
supervisory anchor because datasets such as MAESTRO provide note-aligned velocity labels
through sensor-equipped instruments. For guitar and many other instruments, aligned notes
and audio may be available, but true note-level velocity labels are not.
The chapter addresses this setting through analysis-by-synthesis. A piano-pretrained VeloEst
front end is adapted using differentiable supervision from either a waveform synthesiser or
a SoundFont proxy. The main contribution is Diff-SFProxy: a compact differentiable proxy
that maps note events and predicted velocities to note-wise loudness-related parameters. This
parameter-level interface is more robust than waveform-level supervision under the tested
recording mismatch because it supervises the subset of rendered behaviour most closely related
to velocity. The recovered velocity is renderer-conditioned, but that conditioning is explicit and
operationally useful. It gives a practical route to note-level dynamics recovery when the target
domain lacks direct velocity labels.
7.2 Cross-Chapter Findings
Across the four studies, the main findings are not only architectural. They concern when a
dynamics label is well defined, what evidence can support it, and how it should be evaluated.
7.2.1 Structure is a condition of recovery, not a convenience
All four studies depend on preserving some trusted musical structure. In Chapter 3, this is the
symbolic note sequence. In Chapter 4, it is the aligned score. In Chapter 5, it is the beat and
downbeat grid that makes score dynamics writable. In Chapter 6, it is the aligned note event
list used for renderer-conditioned adaptation. The thesis therefore treats dynamics recovery as
a constrained restoration problem. Without a stable coordinate system, the task collapses into
unconstrained expressive generation.
This stricter formulation is also the source of the practical value. A recovered layer can be
inspected, edited, attached to an existing score, or used to enrich a corpus without changing the
underlying musical content. The preservation constraint is therefore not merely conservative. It
is what makes the output musically accountable.

7.2.2 Velocity and score dynamics are not interchangeable labels
MIDI velocity is a note-level control parameter. Score dynamics is a sparse, contextual marking
system. Perceived loudness is an acoustic or perceptual observation that can support either
task but is not identical to either output. This distinction explains why the thesis uses different
temporal units, losses, and metrics across chapters instead of forcing all dynamics representations
into one scalar target.
This is also why Chapter 6 defines recovered velocity relative to a renderer. Outside piano,
a MIDI velocity value is not a universal physical measurement, but it can still be a useful
note-wise dynamics coordinate for analysis and resynthesis. The thesis therefore avoids the false
choice between physical exactness and musical usefulness. A dynamics representation can be
operationally useful while still being representation-dependent.
7.2.3 Evaluation must match the claimed representation
Point-wise velocity error is useful when ground-truth velocity labels exist, but it can miss
perceptual flatness. Listening tests and dispersion-based diagnostics reveal whether a restored
velocity layer produces audible expressiveness. For score dynamics, beat-synchronous F1 is more
appropriate because the output is a symbolic annotation on a metrical grid. For label-scarce
beyond-piano recovery, perceptual loudness correlation is more defensible than velocity MAE
because there is no ground-truth velocity label in the target domain.
This produces a general evaluation principle: the metric should be tied to the representation
being recovered. A low velocity error is not the same as a convincing score dynamic marking.
A good waveform reconstruction is not the same as accurate velocity recovery. A strong note-
transcription system is not automatically a strong dynamics-transcription system. Treating
these distinctions explicitly is part of the contribution of the thesis.
7.2.4 Weaker supervision changes the claim, not necessarily the
usefulness
The thesis moves from supervised symbolic completion to indirect renderer-conditioned adap-
tation. As supervision weakens, the recovered label should be interpreted more carefully. A
supervised piano velocity estimate can be compared against Disklavier labels. A score-dynamics
estimate can be compared against an editorial annotation convention. A beyond-piano renderer-
conditioned velocity cannot claim to be the performer’s physical gesture, but it can still be a
useful control coordinate for resynthesis, curation, and expressive analysis.
The result is not that weaker supervision makes the task impossible. Rather, weaker
supervision changes what can be claimed. Each claim is valid inside its representational
boundary. The thesis contribution is to make those boundaries explicit instead of hiding them
behind a single generic term such as dynamics.

7. CONCLUSION
7.3 Limitations and Scope
The thesis has several boundaries. These boundaries are not artefacts of unfinished implemen-
tation; they follow from the problem definition and from the representational choices made
throughout the dissertation.
First, the thesis focuses on dynamics rather than on the full space of musical expression.
Timing, articulation, pedalling, timbre, and phrasing interact with dynamics in real performance.
The systems developed here isolate dynamics because it is perceptually salient, directly useful for
data curation, and recoverable under structural preservation. This isolation is methodologically
useful, but it also means that the recovered layer is one dimension of expression rather than a
complete performance model.
Second, the strongest direct supervision in the thesis remains piano-centric. This reflects
the state of available velocity-labelled datasets rather than a belief that piano dynamics is
the universal template for all instruments. Chapter 6 addresses this bias by moving into a
label-scarce guitar setting, but the broader point remains: once the target instrument lacks direct
performance-control labels, the interpretation of recovered velocity must be stated operationally.
Third, score dynamics is inherently contextual. A dynamic marking is not a fixed loudness
threshold. It depends on edition, local musical function, register, texture, and phrase context.
Chapter 5 treats score dynamics as a beat-synchronous symbolic target because that is the
most useful interface for notation and analysis. The resulting labels should therefore be read as
score-facing annotations under a specific annotation convention, not as universal statements
about physical loudness.
Fourth, several chapters assume that structural coordinates are available or recoverable. Score-
informed velocity correction assumes aligned score events. The beyond-piano proxy framework
assumes aligned note events. Audio-to-score dynamics estimates beats and downbeats, but
still relies on the idea that beat-synchronous output is the right symbolic interface. These
assumptions are not weaknesses of the argument. They are the conditions under which label
recovery, rather than unconstrained generation, becomes meaningful.
Finally, the thesis uses multiple forms of evidence rather than a single unified benchmark.
This is necessary because the targets differ across chapters. It also means that the contributions
should be read as a coherent scenario map, not as four entries in one leaderboard. The unifying
object is not one architecture or one dataset. It is the recovery of musically interpretable
dynamics under different structural and supervisory conditions.
7.4 Future Research Directions
The results of the thesis suggest several directions for future research. These directions extend
the thesis argument rather than repair it.

7.4.1 Probabilistic bridges between dynamics representations
A natural direction is to model the relations between velocity, loudness, and score dynamics
probabilistically. The thesis has deliberately avoided fixed lookup tables because they collapse
representation-specific information. A stronger future model could instead treat the three layers
as coupled but uncertain variables. A passage might support several plausible score-dynamic
annotations, several possible velocity profiles, and several loudness realisations depending on
instrument and performance style.
Such a model would allow consistency constraints without enforcing deterministic conversion.
A predicted ff region should normally be compatible with strong local loudness or velocity
evidence, but the relation should be contextual rather than fixed. Conversely, a sequence of
high velocities may indicate a dynamic peak, but only relative to register, texture, and phrase
structure. Probabilistic modelling is a better fit for this many-to-many relation than a scalar
threshold.
7.4.2 Uncertainty-aware score annotation
Score dynamics is a human-readable target, but it is not always a single-label truth. Different
editions and annotators can choose different markings for similar musical evidence. Future
systems should therefore represent uncertainty explicitly. Instead of forcing one label at every
beat, an annotation system could output ranked alternatives, confidence distributions, or
region-level ambiguity flags.
This would be useful in pedagogy and editing. A high-confidence dynamic label could be
written directly into an automatically transcribed score. A low-confidence region could be
presented to a musician for review. In this way, dynamics recovery would become a human-in-
the-loop annotation tool rather than a black-box replacement for editorial judgement.
7.4.3 Instrument-general dynamics coordinates
The beyond-piano problem remains one of the most important directions for expressive AMT.
The central difficulty is not only that guitar, violin, voice, and wind instruments sound different
from piano. It is that their expressive control spaces are different. Piano velocity is a useful
note-onset coordinate. Bowed strings and wind instruments also depend on continuous control
dimensions such as bow pressure, bow speed, breath, vibrato, and articulation.
Future work can generalise the label-recovery stance by defining instrument-appropriate
dynamics coordinates rather than forcing all instruments into piano-style velocity. For some
instruments, the recovered layer may remain a MIDI-like note intensity value. For others, it
may require onset intensity plus sustain shape, articulation class, or continuous modulation
descriptors. The principle from this thesis still applies: the target must be chosen according to
the instrument, the available evidence, and the intended use of the recovered label.

7. CONCLUSION
7.4.4 Corpus-scale expressive data curation
A practical direction is corpus-scale deployment. Many music datasets already contain partial
structure: MIDI files with flat velocity, audio recordings with aligned scores, or symbolic
scores without expressive annotations. The methods in this thesis suggest that these corpora
can be enriched without recollecting them from scratch. Symbolic velocity completion can
improve static MIDI archives. Score-informed correction can refine existing audio–score datasets.
Audio-to-score dynamics can add human-readable markings to performance recordings. Proxy
supervision can extend the approach to label-scarce instruments.
The long-term value of such systems is not only better transcription. It is the construction
of richer musical datasets. Once dynamics labels are recovered consistently, they can support
expressive rendering, performance analysis, pedagogical feedback, and generative modelling.
The thesis therefore positions dynamics recovery as a data infrastructure problem as much as a
modelling problem.
7.4.5 Towards expressive AMT beyond note detection
The broader direction is an AMT paradigm in which note detection is only the first layer. A
musically useful transcription should not stop at pitch and onset. It should also recover the
expressive information that makes the transcription interpretable as performance and usable
as symbolic music data. Dynamics is the layer studied in this thesis, but the same logic can
extend to articulation, pedal, phrasing, and timbre.
The key lesson is that each expressive layer needs its own target definition and coordinate
system. Adding more outputs to a network is not enough. The system must know whether it is
predicting a physical control, a perceptual descriptor, or a score-facing annotation. It must also
evaluate each output in the space where that output has musical meaning. This is the path
from automatic note transcription towards expressive transcription.
7.5 Closing Remarks
This thesis shifts expressive AMT from the question of whether expression can be predicted
to the more precise question of what kind of dynamics label can be recovered under a given
structural and supervisory condition. Its central claim is that dynamics recovery becomes
tractable when the musical structure is preserved, the target representation is explicit, and the
evaluation metric matches the recovered layer.
The resulting view is deliberately constrained. It does not treat expression as a single quantity,
and it does not attempt to rewrite a performance wholesale. Instead, it treats dynamics as an
interpretable layer that can be restored, refined, or constructed from the evidence available.
This provides a practical route for improving weakly expressive MIDI, enriching AMT outputs,
adding score-level dynamic markings, and extending expressive data curation beyond the piano
domain.
