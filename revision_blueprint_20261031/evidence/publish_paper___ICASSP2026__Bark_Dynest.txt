JOINT ESTIMATION OF PIANO DYNAMICS AND METRICAL STRUCTURE WITH A
MULTITASK MULTI-SCALE CNN
Zhanhong He1, Hanyu Meng2, David Huang1, Roberto Togneri1
1The University of Western Australia, Perth, Australia
2The University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia
ABSTRACT
Estimating piano dynamic from audio recordings is a fundamental
challenge in computational music analysis. In this paper, we pro-
pose an efficient multitask CNN that jointly predicts dynamic levels,
change points, beats, and downbeats from a shared latent represen-
tation. These four targets form the metrical structure of dynamics in
the music score. Inspired by recent vocal dynamic research, we use
the multi-scale CNN backbone to leverage Bark-scale specific loud-
ness as input features. Compared to log-Mel as input, this reduces
model size from 13.4 M to 0.5 M, enabling long audio sequential
input. We indentify the 60-second as optimial for combined tasks,
which doubled the length of beat tracking commonly used. Evalu-
ated on the public MazurkaBL dataset, our model achieves state-of-
the-art results across all tasks. This work sets a new benchmark for
piano dynamic estimation and delivers a powerful and compact tool,
paving the way for large-scale, resource-efficient analysis of musical
expression.

Given these complexities, we propose an end-to-end multitask
learning approach to estimate dynamic markings and their under-
lying metrical structure directly from audio. Inspired by recent ad-
vances in vocal dynamics estimation [12], we use Bark-scale specific
loudness (BSSL) as input feature. This representation is processed
by a multi-scale attention CNN backbone, adapted from [13], to ex-
tract a shared latent representation. This unified representation is
then channeled through a Multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts (MMoE)
layer [14], which generates specialized features for four distinct task
heads that jointly predict: (1) dynamic levels, (2) change points, (3)
beat positions, and (4) downbeat positions. Together, these targets
model not only the dynamic markings but also their underlying met-
rical structure, as the beat and downbeat grid forms the rhythmic
foundation of a musical score.
The proposed multitask framework offers significant practical
applications compared to single-task approaches. Its primary use is
to enrich a musical score with dynamic markings, which is particu-
larly valuable for enhancing the output of score-level automatic mu-
sic transcription systems that typically capture only musical notes.
This is achieved by combining the model’s predicted dynamic levels
and change points and aligning them to a beat grid (see Fig. 1). A
more advanced application stems from the model’s end-to-end de-
sign, where it can predict the beat and downbeat grid by itself, using
these internal predictions to post-process the dynamics. This enables
fully audio-based music analysis where no ground-truth score or an-
notations are available. By achieving state-of-the-art results on the
expressive MazurkaBL dataset, we present this multitask CNN as
a complete, end-to-end tool that supports large-scale music perfor-
mance analysis, making it a valuable resource for the music research
community.1
1. INTRODUCTION
The creation, comprehension and reproduction of music are fun-
damental aspects of human culture. In western musical tradition,
dynamics-the variations in loudness-is a key aspect of musical ex-
pression [1]. Indicated in a score by markings such as p (piano,
soft) and f (forte, loud), dynamics are essential for shaping musical
phrases, conveying emotion, and articulating structural differenta-
tion. This vocabulary extended to a nuanced of static levels, from pp
(pianissimo) to ff (fortissimo), and includes gradual transitions like
crescendo and decrescendo. The computational modeling of these
expressive markings is valuable for music education, performance
analysis, and theory-informed music generation [2].
The core challenge in estimation from audio lies in the inherent
relativity and ambiguity of dynamic markings. A symbol like pianis-
simo does not map to a fixed physical measurement like a decibel
level. Instead, its interpretation is deeply contextual, influenced by
musical style, performer’s intent, and the acoustic environment [3,4].
This lack of a standardized ground truth has historically posed a sig-
nificant challenge for machine learning algorithms, often leading to
poor generalization among different performers or pieces [5, 6].
To circumvent the ambiguity of music dynamics, researchers of-
ten adopt proxy targets: LUFS in audio generation and MIDI veloc-
ity in music transcription and analysis [7–9]. However, MIDI ve-
locity reflects performer’s physical action rather than the perceived
loudness, and is entangled with music instrumental timbre and touch
[10]. Although automatic music transcription (AMT) systems can

2.2. Model Architecture
The proposed model is illustrated in Fig. 2. With divergent acous-
tic requirements, dynamic estimation benefits from a large tempo-
ral receptive field [12], whereas beat and downbeat tracking require
high temporal resolution to locate transient onsets [24]. To address
this need for varied receptive fields, we adapt the multi-scale CNN
from [13] as a shared encoder. Given an F ×T time–frequency fea-
ture as input, the encoder operates on three parallel streams at dif-
ferent temporal resolutions, corresponding to lengths of T , T /s, and
T /s2. The downsampling and upsampling by the scaling factor s
are implemented via setting strides in max-pooling and transposed
convolutions. Unlike the original design [13], which used a fix fac-
tor of 3, we treat the scaling factors as hyperparameters and study
task-dependencies via ablations. We also replace the original task-
specific classifiers with a shared latent sequence Z ∈RT ×8 con-
sumed by all tasks. The model employs a compact configuration
with a shared latent dimension of 8, a temporal scaling factor of 5,
and an MMoE block with 8 experts.
To specialize the shared representation and mitigate negative
transfer between the acoustically heterogeneous tasks (multi-class

3. EXPERIMENTS
2.3. Task-specific post-processing
The model’s raw output is a sequence of frame-wise probabili-
ties, which we convert into discrete musical events via tailored
post-processing. For beat and downbeat detection, we follow [19],
identifying events by thresholding probabilities at 50% and applying
peak-picking within a 70 ms neighborhood (±3 frames at 50 fps).
Our final target, a set of discrete dynamic markings, is obtained
by sampling the continuous dynamic level (a curve) at the precise lo-
cation of each change point. We detect these two intermediate com-
ponents from the model’s output: the dynamic level is determined by
an arg max over class probabilities, and change points are selected
by peak-picking on probabilities exceeding a 75% threshold.
An additional post-processing step is applied to change points.
While it is not a strict musical rule that dynamic changes occur ex-
actly on beat positions (they can occur between beats), the anno-
tations in the MazurkaBL dataset are exclusively at beat locations.
To be consistent with prior work [25], we snap the detected change
points to the nearest beat position to ensure a comparable evaluation.
3.1. Dataset
This experiment uses the MazurkaBL dataset [6], a score-aligned
corpus of 2,098 solo piano recordings covering 44 Chopin Mazurkas.
After excluding two opuses with irregular dynamics annotations,
1,999 recordings remained. MazurkaBL is the largest resource for
studying notated dynamics versus performed loudness from audio,
providing score-aligned beat times and verified expressive markings,
with a specific focus on dynamics. In contrast, other large datasets
like MAESTRO [26] use MIDI velocity as a proxy for dynamics,
while others such as ASAP [27] provide score-aligned annotations
but lack dynamic markings.
3.2. Implementation details
We use a 5-fold cross-validation protocol, stratified by Mazurka
opus, to train and evaluate our model. Within each fold, the model is
trained for 120 epochs, with the best-performing checkpoint selected
based on the F1 score on its respective validation set. The training
configuration includes the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of
3e-4, a batch size of 10, and a fixed random seed of 86. For data
augmentation, we slice audio into 60-second segments with 50%
overlap during training, while no overlap is used for evaluation.
Both BSSL (22 bark bands) and log-Mel (128 mel bins) features
are extracted using the same STFT parameters as in [19] to ensure
identical temporal resolution. When run on a NVIDIA RTX 3090
(24 GiB), a full 5-fold run completed in approximately 20 hours,
with peak GPU memory usage of 4 GiB.

4. RESULTS
3.4. Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate four tasks using F1 scores. For beat and downbeat track-
ing, we report F1 with a ±70 ms tolerance, consistent with prior
work [19, 24]. For dynamic levels and change points, we devise two
distinct evaluation scenarios.
Basic. This scenario simulates an ideal case where accurate beat in-
formation is available, such as refinement to a music score that has
reliable beat positions but lacks dynamic markings. Consistent with
single-task benchmarks [25], we align the model’s predictions with
the ground-truth beats. The continuous dynamic levels are sampled
at these ground-truth positions, discretized, and then evaluated us-
ing a macro-averaged F1 score across five classes (pp, p, mf, f, ff ),
the sixth class blank is masked). Change points are snapped to the
nearest ground-truth beat and are thus represented on the beat grid
for evaluation with a standard F1 score.
Advanced. This scenario assesses the model’s practical, end-to-end
performance where ground-truth beats are unavailable, such as in
audio-only music analysis. The evaluation relies on the model’s own
predicted beat positions. We first sample the predicted dynamic level
curve at these predicted beats to obtain a beat-wise sequence of dy-
namics. Then, we achor the predicted change points to this beat grid.
The final ”dynamic markings” are formed by using these change
point indices to define the dynamic level for each segment. This
output is evaluated using accuracy (Acc) and the micro-averaged F1
score across five dynamic classes .
The proposed multitask CNN model demonstrates superior perfor-
mance across all four tasks in Table 1. It achieves new SOTA F1
scores for both dynamics estimation (51.4%) and change point de-
tection (21.6%). Although a small gap remains in beat tracking when
compared with the specialized system ”Beat This”, the core advan-
tage of our approach is confirmed by the comparison with single-task
CNNs. With BSSL features, the multitask CNN achieves improve-
ments in dynamics (+3.7%), change points (+4.8%), and downbeats
(+8.2%), with only a minor trade-off in beat detection (-0.3%). No-
tably, this overall performance is achieved within a single, highly
parameter-efficient multitask model that further confirms its prac-
tical benefits, as it uses its own predicted beat positions for post-
processing rather than relying on ground-truth annotations.
Beyond the performance gains, our analysis reveals a strong
task-feature dependency, with BSSL features proving superior for
dynamics and log-Mel features excelling at beat tracking. The key
advantage of BSSL is its compactness (22 Bark bins in our FFT set-
tings, compared to 128 Mel bins), which dramatically reduces the
model size from 13.5 M to 0.4 M trainable parameters. This smaller
footprint enables the processing of longer audio sequences, directly
benefiting tasks that require long-term temporal information and re-
vealing BSSL’s potential for a wider range of musical tasks with
long-temporal dependencies.
Furthermore, our ablation study (in Table 1) investigates the im-
pact of the MMoE module and the multi-scale functionality. Re-
moving the MMoE module significantly degrades dynamics estima-
tion while yielding minor gains in other tasks, highlighting its role in
optimally allocating weights by light-weight shared experts, instead
of a huge structural model changes. Disabling the multi-scale func-
tionality (by setting the scaling factor s = 1) impairs the model’s
ability to perceive features at different temporal resolutions, leading
to a performance drop across all tasks. This degradation was partic-
ularly severe in change point and downbeat detection, which might
requiring high temporal precision.

5. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we introduce a compact multitask, multi-scale CNN
that jointly estimates piano dynamics, change points, beats, and
downbeats directly from audio. Using Bark-scale specific loudness
as input and an MMoE decoder, our model leverages long-range
temporal context (60 s) while remaining highly parameter-efficient
(0.5 M). Evaluated on the MazurkaBL dataset, it achieves SOTA
results for dynamics and change point detection and competitive in
beat and downbeat tracking. This demonstrates not only the model’s
practical utility but also its significant potential for broader appli-
cations. Future work will focus on combining our proposed model
with score-level piano transcription systems. Such a pipeline would
produce music scores with expressive dynamics marking more than
pp to ff, and though this presents a new challenge in developing
appropriate evaluation methods for such comprehensive outputs.
